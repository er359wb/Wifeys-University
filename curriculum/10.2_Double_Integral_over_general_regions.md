## 1. From a rectangle to a general region (slides 2-3)

We want to integrate $f$ not only over rectangles but also over regions $D$ of
more general shape, with $D$ a **bounded** region, that is, $D$ can be enclosed
in a rectangle $R$ (slide 2). Define a new function $F$ on $R$:
$$F(x,y)=\begin{cases}f(x,y),&(x,y)\text{ in }D\\0,&(x,y)\text{ in }R\text{ but not in }D\end{cases}$$
If $F$ is integrable over $R$, the **double integral of $f$ over $D$** is
$$\iint_Df(x,y)\,dA=\iint_RF(x,y)\,dA$$
(slide 3: the procedure is reasonable because $F=0$ outside $D$, so those
points contribute nothing to the integral).

## 2. Type I and Type II regions (slides 4-8)

**Type I (slide 4).** The region lies between two curves $y=g_1(x)$ and
$y=g_2(x)$ over an interval $[a,b]$:
$$D=\{(x,y)\mid a\le x\le b,\ g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)\}$$
with $g_1,g_2$ continuous on $[a,b]$. ($y$ goes from the lower curve to the
upper curve.)

**Evaluating over Type I (slides 5-6).** Choose a rectangle $R=[a,b]\times[c,d]$
that contains $D$ and let $F$ be as above. Then
$\iint_Df\,dA=\iint_RF\,dA=\int_a^b\!\int_c^dF(x,y)\,dy\,dx$. Observe that
$F(x,y)=0$ if $y<g_1(x)$ or $y>g_2(x)$ because $(x,y)$ then lies outside $D$, so
$\int_c^dF(x,y)\,dy=\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}F\,dy=\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy$.

**Formula for Type I:** if $f$ is continuous on a type I region
$D=\{(x,y)\mid a\le x\le b,\ g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)\}$, then
$$\iint_Df(x,y)\,dA=\int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\int_a^b\left(\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx$$

**Type II (slide 7).** The region lies between two curves $x=h_1(y)$ and
$x=h_2(y)$ over an interval $[c,d]$ of $y$:
$$D=\{(x,y)\mid c\le y\le d,\ h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y)\}$$
with $h_1,h_2$ continuous on $[c,d]$. ($x$ goes from the left curve to the right
curve.)

**Formula for Type II (slide 8):**
$$\iint_Df(x,y)\,dA=\int_c^d\!\!\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\int_c^d\left(\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)}f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy$$

**Note on diagrams (slide 14).** When setting up a double integral, it is
essential to **draw a diagram**. Often it is helpful to draw a vertical (or
horizontal) arrow: the arrow starts at the lower (left) boundary, which gives
the lower limit of the inner integral, and ends at the upper (right) boundary,
which gives the upper limit. A vertical arrow goes with Type I ($dy\,dx$), a
horizontal arrow with Type II ($dx\,dy$).

## 3. Examples (slides 9-19)

**Example 1 (slides 9-10).** Evaluate $\iint_D(2x+y)\,dA$ as printed in the
statement, where $D$ is the region bounded by the parabolas $y=2x^2$ and
$y=x^2+1$. The parabolas intersect when $x=1$ and $x=-1$. Type I region:
$D=\{(x,y)\mid-1\le x\le1,\ 2x^2\le y\le1+x^2\}$ (lower boundary $y=2x^2$, upper
boundary $y=1+x^2$).

**The slide's solution actually integrates $x+2y$, not $2x+y$** (checked by hand
and numerically: the integral of $x+2y$ over this region is $32/15\approx2.1333$,
the integral of $2x+y$ would be $16/15\approx1.0667$, half as much). The slide's
working:
$$\iint_D(x+2y)\,dA=\int_{-1}^1\!\int_{2x^2}^{1+x^2}(x+2y)\,dy\,dx=\int_{-1}^1\big[xy+y^2\big]_{y=2x^2}^{y=1+x^2}dx$$
$$=\int_{-1}^1(-3x^4-x^3+2x^2+x+1)\,dx=\frac{32}{15}$$
If this example is taught, use $x+2y$ (the slide's answer belongs to it).

**Example 2 (slides 11-13).** Evaluate $\iint_Dxy\,dA$, where $D$ is the region
bounded by the parabola $y^2=2x+6$ and the line $y=x-1$. The two curves
intersect when $x=-1$ and $x=5$, at the points $(-1,-2)$ and $(5,4)$. Slide 12
asks **"Type I or Type II?"** and shows both: (a) as a Type I region, the lower
boundary consists of **two parts** ($y=-\sqrt{2x+6}$ for $-3\le x\le-1$, then the
line $y=x-1$), so it has to be split in two; (b) as a Type II region, with
$x=\frac{y^2}{2}-3$ on the left and $x=y+1$ on the right, one integral is
enough. Slide 13 (Type II):
$$D=\{(x,y)\mid-2\le y\le4,\ \tfrac12y^2-3\le x\le y+1\}$$
$$\iint_Dxy\,dA=\int_{-2}^4\!\int_{\frac12y^2-3}^{y+1}xy\,dx\,dy=\int_{-2}^4\left[\frac{x^2}{2}y\right]_{x=\frac12y^2-3}^{x=y+1}dy=\frac12\int_{-2}^4y\Big[(y+1)^2-\big(\tfrac12y^2-3\big)^2\Big]dy$$
$$=\frac12\left[-\frac{y^6}{24}+y^4+2\frac{y^3}{3}-4y^2\right]_{-2}^4=36$$
(Checked by hand: $\int_{-2}^4$ of the bracket's integrand equals $72$, half of it is $36$.)

**Example 3 (slides 15-17).** Find the volume of the solid that lies under the
paraboloid $z=x^2+y^2$ and above the region $D$ in the $xy$-plane bounded by the
line $y=2x$ and the parabola $y=x^2$. The curves meet at $(0,0)$ and $(2,4)$.

- As a **Type I** region (slide 16): the printed description is
  $D=\{(x,y)\mid0\le x\le2,\ x^2\le x\le2x\}$, a typo; it means
  $x^2\le y\le2x$. Then
  $V=\int_0^2\!\int_{x^2}^{2x}(x^2+y^2)\,dy\,dx=\int_0^2\Big(-\frac{x^6}{3}-x^4+\frac{14x^3}{3}\Big)dx=\Big[-\frac{x^7}{21}-\frac{x^5}{5}+\frac{7x^4}{6}\Big]_0^2=\frac{216}{35}$.
- As a **Type II** region (slide 17):
  $D=\{(x,y)\mid0\le y\le4,\ \tfrac12y\le x\le\sqrt y\}$ and
  $V=\int_0^4\!\int_{y/2}^{\sqrt y}(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy=\int_0^4\Big(\frac{y^{3/2}}{3}+y^{5/2}-\frac{y^3}{24}-\frac{y^3}{2}\Big)dy=\Big[\frac{2}{15}y^{5/2}+\frac27y^{7/2}-\frac{13}{96}y^4\Big]_0^4=\frac{216}{35}$.
  Both orders give the same volume (checked).

**Example 4 (slides 18-19): changing the order of integration.** Evaluate
$\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\int_x^1\sin(y^2)\,dy\,dx$. If we try it as it stands we
must first evaluate $\int_x^1\sin(y^2)\,dy$, which is impossible in finite terms
($\sin(y^2)$ has no elementary antiderivative), so we must **change the order of
integration**. The region is
$D=\{(x,y)\mid0\le x\le1,\ x\le y\le1\}=\{(x,y)\mid0\le y\le1,\ 0\le x\le y\}$:
$$\int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1\sin(y^2)\,dy\,dx=\iint_D\sin(y^2)\,dA=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^y\sin(y^2)\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1y\sin(y^2)\,dy=-\frac12\cos(y^2)\Big|_0^1=\frac12(1-\cos1)$$

## 4. Properties (slide 20)

- $\iint_D[f+g]\,dA=\iint_Df\,dA+\iint_Dg\,dA$;
- $\iint_Dcf\,dA=c\iint_Df\,dA$;
- if $f\ge g$ for all $(x,y)$ in $D$, then $\iint_Df\,dA\ge\iint_Dg\,dA$;
- if $D=D_1\cup D_2$, where $D_1$ and $D_2$ do not overlap except perhaps on
  their boundaries, then $\iint_Df\,dA=\iint_{D_1}f\,dA+\iint_{D_2}f\,dA$.
