## 1. Recall 2-D coordinate systems (slide 2)

A point is $(a,b)$: $a$ is the $x$-coordinate, $b$ is the $y$-coordinate. The
$x$-axis is horizontal, the $y$-axis is vertical, $O$ is the origin. A plane is
called two-dimensional.

## 2. 3-D coordinate systems (slides 3-8)

**Axes (slide 3).** The $x$-axis and $y$-axis are horizontal, the $z$-axis is
vertical, $O$ is the origin. The direction of the $z$-axis is determined by the
**right-hand rule**: curl the fingers of your right hand around the $z$-axis in
the direction of a $90^\circ$ counterclockwise rotation from the positive
$x$-axis to the positive $y$-axis; the thumb points along the positive
$z$-axis.

**Coordinate planes and octants (slide 4).** Three coordinate planes: the
$xy$-plane, the $yz$-plane and the $xz$-plane. They divide space into eight
octants, labeled I to VIII on the slide (the slide does not say which octant is
which).

**Locating the point $(a,b,c)$ (slide 5).** First move $a$ units along the
$x$-axis, then $b$ units parallel to the $y$-axis, finally $c$ units parallel
to the $z$-axis. The figure shows the box with corners $S(a,0,c)$, $R(0,b,c)$,
$Q(a,b,0)$ and the point $P(a,b,c)$.

**Cartesian product (slide 5).**
$\mathbb R\times\mathbb R\times\mathbb R=\{(x,y,z)\mid x,y,z\in\mathbb R\}=\mathbb R^3$,
the set of all ordered triples of real numbers. Points $P$ in space correspond
one-to-one to ordered triples $(a,b,c)$ in $\mathbb R^3$.

**3-D analytic geometry (slides 6-8).** In 2-D, the graph of an equation in $x$
and $y$ is a curve in $\mathbb R^2$ (e.g. $y=x^2-1$ is a parabola, $y-x+2=0$ is
a straight line). In 3-D, the graph of an equation in $x,y,z$ is a surface in
$\mathbb R^3$. Example (slides 6-7): $y=5$ is a line in $\mathbb R^2$ but a
plane in $\mathbb R^3$; $x=-1$ is likewise a plane.

**Note (slide 8).** If $k$ is a constant: $x=k$ is a plane parallel to the
$yz$-plane; $y=k$ is a plane parallel to the $xz$-plane; $z=k$ is a plane
parallel to the $xy$-plane. The three coordinate planes: $x=0$ is the
$yz$-plane, $y=0$ is the $xz$-plane, $z=0$ is the $xy$-plane.

## 3. Distance formula in three dimensions (slides 9-13)

**Distance formula (slide 9).** The distance between
$P_1(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ and $P_2(x_2,y_2,z_2)$ is
$$|P_1P_2|=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2+(z_2-z_1)^2}$$
Proof (slide 9, with the box in the figure, $A(x_2,y_1,z_1)$ and
$B(x_2,y_2,z_1)$): $|P_1A|=|x_2-x_1|$, $|AB|=|y_2-y_1|$, $|BP_2|=|z_2-z_1|$.
Pythagoras twice: $|P_1B|^2=|P_1A|^2+|AB|^2$ and
$|P_1P_2|^2=|P_1B|^2+|BP_2|^2$, which gives the formula.

**Example 1 (slide 10).** Find the distance from $P(2,-1,7)$ to $Q(1,-3,5)$.
$$|PQ|=\sqrt{(1-2)^2+(-3+1)^2+(5-7)^2}=\sqrt{1+4+4}=3$$

**Example 2 (slide 10).** Find an equation of a sphere with radius $r$ and
center $C(x_0,y_0,z_0)$. By definition a sphere is the set of all points
$P(x,y,z)$ whose distance from $C$ is $r$. $P$ is on the sphere iff $|PC|=r$:
$\sqrt{(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2+(z-z_0)^2}=r$, so
$$(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2+(z-z_0)^2=r^2$$

**Equation of a sphere (slide 11).** Center $C(x_0,y_0,z_0)$, radius $r$:
$(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2+(z-z_0)^2=r^2$. If the center is the origin:
$x^2+y^2+z^2=r^2$.

**Example 3 (slide 11).** Show that $x^2+y^2+z^2+4x-6y+2z+6=0$ is the equation
of a sphere and find its center and radius. Complete the squares:
$(x^2+4x+4)+(y^2-6y+9)+(z^2+2z+1)=-6+4+9+1$, so
$(x+2)^2+(y-3)^2+(z+1)^2=8$. Center $(-2,3,-1)$, radius $r=\sqrt8=2\sqrt2$.

**Example 4 (slide 12).** What region in $\mathbb R^3$ is represented by
$1\le x^2+y^2+z^2\le4,\ z\ge0$? Rewritten:
$1\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\le2$, so the points $(x,y,z)$ whose distance from the
origin is at least 1 and at most 2: between (or on) the spheres
$x^2+y^2+z^2=1$ and $x^2+y^2+z^2=4$. Then the slide adds the condition on $z$
and concludes "the points lie on or below the $xy$-plane" (figure: the lower
half of the spherical shell). Slide inconsistency: it prints the condition as
$z\ge0$, but "on or below the $xy$-plane" (and the figure) correspond to
$z\le0$; $z\ge0$ would be the upper half. The standard version of this example
uses $z\le0$.
