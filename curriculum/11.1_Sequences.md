## 1. What a sequence is (slide 2)

A sequence can be thought of as a list of numbers written in a definite order:
$$a_1,\ a_2,\ a_3,\ \dots,\ a_{i-1},\ a_i,\ a_{i+1},\ \dots,\ a_n,\ \dots$$
$a_1$ is the first term, $a_i$ the $i$th term; each term $a_n$ has a successor
$a_{n+1}$. For every positive integer $n$ there is a corresponding number
$a_n=f(n)$, $n\in\mathbb N$, so a sequence can be defined as a **function whose
domain is the set of positive integers**; we usually write $a_n$ instead of
$f(n)$. Notation: $\{a_n\}$ or $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$.

## 2. Examples of sequences (slides 3-4)

**Example 1 (slide 3).** $n$ does not have to start at $1$. Each sequence is
shown in three forms: the notation, the defining formula (general term $a_n$),
and the list of terms.

- (a) $\left\{\dfrac n{n+1}\right\}_{n=1}^\infty$, $a_n=\dfrac n{n+1}$:
  $\dfrac12,\dfrac23,\dfrac34,\dots,\dfrac{n-1}n,\dfrac n{n+1},\dots$
- (b) $\left\{\dfrac{(-1)^n(n+1)}{3^n}\right\}_{n=1}^\infty$:
  $-\dfrac23,\dfrac39,-\dfrac4{27},\dfrac5{81},\dots,\dfrac{(-1)^n(n+1)}{3^n},\dots$
- (c) $\{\sqrt{n-3}\}_{n=3}^\infty$, $a_n=\sqrt{n-3}$:
  $0,1,\sqrt2,\dots,\sqrt{n-3},\dots$ (here $n$ starts at $3$).

**Example 2 (slide 4).** Sequences that do not have a simple equation:
(a) $a_n$ is the population of a country in year $n$; (b) $a_n$ is the digit in
the $n$th decimal place of the number $e$: $\{7,1,8,2,8,1,8,2,8,4,5,\dots\}$;
(c) the **Fibonacci sequence**, defined recursively by $a_1=1$, $a_2=1$,
$a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}$ for $n\ge3$ (each term is the sum of the two preceding
terms): $1,1,2,3,5,8,13,21,\dots$

## 3. Limit of a sequence (slides 5-7)

**Definition (slide 5).** A sequence $\{a_n\}$ has the limit $L$, and we write
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L\quad\text{or}\quad a_n\to L\text{ as }n\to\infty$$
if we can take the terms $a_n$ as close to $L$ as we like by taking $n$
sufficiently large. If $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n$ exists, the sequence **converges**
(is convergent). Otherwise it **diverges** (is divergent). Picture on the slide:
$\left\{\dfrac n{n+1}\right\}$, terms $\dfrac12,\dfrac23,\dfrac34,\dots$ approach
a number, $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac n{n+1}=1$.

**Example 3 (slides 6-7).** Is $\left\{\dfrac n{n+1}\right\}$ convergent or
divergent? The slide's solution: since $\lim\dfrac n{n+1}=L$ (a number; its value
$1$ is from slide 5), the sequence is **convergent**. (Why the limit is $1$:
divide numerator and denominator by $n$: $\dfrac n{n+1}=\dfrac1{1+1/n}\to1$.)

**Example 4 (slide 6-7).** Is $a_n=\dfrac n{\sqrt{10+n}}$ convergent or
divergent? Divide numerator and denominator by $n$:
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac n{\sqrt{10+n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1{\sqrt{\frac{10}{n^2}+\frac1n}}=\infty$$
The limit does not exist, so $\{a_n\}$ is **divergent**. Remark on the slide:
if $\lim a_n=\infty$, the sequence is divergent but in a special way; we say
$\{a_n\}$ **diverges to $\infty$**.

## 4. Limit laws (slides 8-10)

**Limit laws (slide 8).** If $\{a_n\}$ and $\{b_n\}$ are convergent sequences and
$c$ is a constant, then
- $\lim(a_n+b_n)=\lim a_n+\lim b_n$ and $\lim(a_n-b_n)=\lim a_n-\lim b_n$;
- $\lim ca_n=c\lim a_n$ and $\lim a_nb_n=\lim a_n\cdot\lim b_n$;
- $\lim\dfrac{a_n}{b_n}=\dfrac{\lim a_n}{\lim b_n}$ if $\lim b_n\ne0$;
- $\lim(a_n)^p=[\lim a_n]^p$ if $p>0$ and $a_n>0$.

**Squeeze Theorem (slides 9-10).** If $a_n\le b_n\le c_n$ for all $n\ge N$ and
$\lim a_n=\lim c_n=L$, then $\lim b_n=L$ (the sequence $\{b_n\}$ is squeezed
between $\{a_n\}$ and $\{c_n\}$).

**Another useful fact (slides 9-10).** If $\lim|a_n|=0$, then $\lim a_n=0$.

**Example 5 (slide 10).** Is $a_n=\dfrac{(-1)^n}n$ convergent or divergent?
Since $\lim\left|\dfrac{(-1)^n}n\right|=\lim\dfrac1n=0$, we have
$\lim\dfrac{(-1)^n}n=0$. So the sequence is **convergent**. (Slide 9 is the same
slide without the solution.)

## 5. Bounded sequences (slide 11)

A sequence $\{a_n\}$ is **bounded above** if there is a number $M$ such that
$a_n\le M$ for all $n\ge1$; **bounded below** if there is a number $m$ such that
$a_n\ge m$ for all $n\ge1$; if it is bounded above and below, it is a **bounded
sequence**.

- $\{n^2\}$ is bounded below, since $n^2\ge1$ for $n\ge1$ (not bounded above).
- $\left\{\sin\dfrac\pi{n+1}\right\}$ is bounded: $0\le\sin\dfrac\pi{n+1}\le1$ for
  $n\ge1$. The slide adds that it is also monotonic (decreasing), and that a
  **bounded monotonic sequence is convergent** (boxed on the slide, "bounded,
  monotonic, convergent"; "monotonic" is used without being defined, and the
  statement is shown without proof).
