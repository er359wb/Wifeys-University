## 1. Lines in space (slides 2-7)

**What determines a line (slide 2).** A point $P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ on the line
$L$ and the direction of $L$. A vector $\vec v$ parallel to $L$ is a
direction vector of $L$.

**Parametric equations (slide 3).** $P_0$ is a given point on $L$,
$\vec v=\langle a,b,c\rangle$ a direction vector, $P(x,y,z)$ an arbitrary point
of $L$. Then $\vec{P_0P}=\langle x-x_0,y-y_0,z-z_0\rangle$ is parallel to
$\vec v$, i.e. $\vec{P_0P}=\lambda\vec v$, which gives
$$\begin{cases}x=x_0+\lambda a\\y=y_0+\lambda b\\z=z_0+\lambda c\end{cases}\quad\lambda\in\mathbb R$$
the parametric equations of the line through $P_0$ parallel to $\vec v=\langle a,b,c\rangle$.

**Example 1 (slide 4).** (a) Find parametric equations for the line through
$(5,1,3)$ parallel to $\vec i+4\vec j-2\vec k$. (b) Find two other points on the
line. Solution: $P_0(5,1,3)$, $\vec v=\langle1,4,-2\rangle$, so
$x=5+\lambda,\ y=1+4\lambda,\ z=3-2\lambda$. (b) $\lambda=1$ gives $(6,5,1)$;
$\lambda=-1$ gives $(4,-3,5)$. Remark: the parametric equations of a line are
not unique.

**Symmetric equations (slides 5-6).** If $a\ne0$, $b\ne0$, $c\ne0$, solving
each parametric equation for $\lambda$ gives
$$\frac{x-x_0}{a}=\frac{y-y_0}{b}=\frac{z-z_0}{c}$$
If one of $a,b,c$ is 0, e.g. $a=0$: $x=x_0$ and
$\dfrac{y-y_0}{b}=\dfrac{z-z_0}{c}$; the line lies in the vertical plane
$x=x_0$. If two are 0, e.g. $a=b=0$: $x=x_0,\ y=y_0$; the line is parallel to
the $z$-axis.

**Example 2 (slide 7).** (a) Find parametric equations and symmetric equations
for the line through $A(2,4,-3)$ and $B(3,-1,1)$. (b) At what point does this
line intersect the $xy$-plane? The slide gives only the general fact: if the
line passes through $P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ and $P_1(x_1,y_1,z_1)$, a direction
vector is $\vec v=\langle x_1-x_0,y_1-y_0,z_1-z_0\rangle$, and the symmetric
equation is $\dfrac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}=\dfrac{y-y_0}{y_1-y_0}=\dfrac{z-z_0}{z_1-z_0}$.
(No solution on the slide.)

## 2. Planes in space (slides 8-17)

**What determines a plane (slide 8).** A single vector parallel to a plane is
not enough to give its "direction", but a vector perpendicular to the plane
does. So a plane is determined by a point $P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ in the plane and a
vector $\vec n=\langle A,B,C\rangle$ orthogonal to the plane, called a **normal
vector**.

**Equation of a plane (slide 9).** $P(x,y,z)$ is on the plane iff
$\vec{P_0P}\perp\vec n$, i.e. $\vec{P_0P}\cdot\vec n=0$ with
$\vec{P_0P}=\langle x-x_0,y-y_0,z-z_0\rangle$:
$$A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0\qquad(\text{vector equation of the plane})$$

**Linear equation (slide 11).** Expanding gives $Ax+By+Cz+D=0$ with
$D=-Ax_0-By_0-Cz_0$. Conversely, if $A,B,C$ are not all 0, then
$Ax+By+Cz+D=0$ represents a plane with normal vector $\langle A,B,C\rangle$.

**Example 3 (slide 10).** (a) Find an equation of the plane through
$(2,4,-1)$ with normal vector $\vec n=\langle2,3,4\rangle$. (b) Find the
intercepts and sketch the plane. (a) $2(x-2)+3(y-4)+4(z+1)=0$, or
$2x+3y+4z=12$. (b) The $x$-intercept: set $y=z=0$, get $x=6$; similarly the
$y$-intercept is 4 and the $z$-intercept is 3.

**Example 4 (slide 12).** Find an equation of the plane through $P(1,3,2)$,
$Q(3,-1,6)$, $R(5,2,0)$. $\vec{PQ}=\langle2,-4,4\rangle$,
$\vec{PR}=\langle4,-1,-2\rangle$. Both lie in the plane, so their cross product
is orthogonal to the plane and can be taken as the normal vector:
$\vec n=\vec{PQ}\times\vec{PR}=\langle12,20,14\rangle$. The plane:
$12(x-1)+20(y-3)+14(z-2)=0$, or $6x+10y+7z=50$.

**Parallel and nonparallel planes (slide 13).** Two planes are parallel if
their normal vectors are parallel. Examples: $2x+3y+4z=12$ and $2x+3y+4z=-5$
(both $\vec n=\langle2,3,4\rangle$) and $-2x-3y-4z=0$
($\vec n=\langle-2,-3,-4\rangle$). Nonparallel planes intersect in a straight
line, and the angle between the two planes is defined as the acute angle between
their normal vectors.

**Example 5 (slides 14-15).** (a) Find the angle between the planes $x+y+z=1$
and $x-2y+3z=1$. (b) Find symmetric equations for their line of intersection
$L$.
(a) $\vec n_1=\langle1,1,1\rangle$, $\vec n_2=\langle1,-2,3\rangle$:
$$\cos\theta=\frac{\vec n_1\cdot\vec n_2}{|\vec n_1||\vec n_2|}=\frac{1(1)+1(-2)+1(3)}{\sqrt{1+1+1}\sqrt{1+4+9}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{42}},\quad\theta=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac2{\sqrt{42}}\right)\approx72^\circ$$
(b) First find a point on $L$: set $z=0$ in both equations: $x+y=1$ and
$x-2y=1$, so $x=1$, $y=0$; the point $(1,0,0)$ lies on $L$. $L$ lies in both
planes, so it is perpendicular to both normal vectors; a direction vector is
$\vec v=\vec n_1\times\vec n_2=\langle1,1,1\rangle\times\langle1,-2,3\rangle=\langle5,-2,-3\rangle$.
Symmetric equations: $\dfrac{x-1}{5}=\dfrac{y}{-2}=\dfrac{z}{-3}$.

**Example 6 (slide 16).** Find a formula for the distance $d$ from a point
$P_1(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ to the plane $ax+by+cz+d=0$ (the slide uses the letter $d$
both for the distance and for the constant term, as printed). Let
$P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ be any point in the plane, $\vec b=\vec{P_0P_1}=\langle x_1-x_0,y_1-y_0,z_1-z_0\rangle$,
$\vec n=\langle a,b,c\rangle$. Then the distance is the absolute value of the
scalar projection of $\vec b$ onto $\vec n$:
$$d=\left|\operatorname{comp}_{\vec n}\vec b\right|=\frac{|\vec n\cdot\vec b|}{|\vec n|}=\frac{|a(x_1-x_0)+b(y_1-y_0)+c(z_1-z_0)|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}=\frac{|ax_1+by_1+cz_1+d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}$$
(using $ax_0+by_0+cz_0=-d$ since $P_0$ is in the plane).

**Example 7 (slide 17).** Find the distance between the parallel planes
$10x+2y-2z=5$ and $5x+y-z=1$. The planes are parallel because their normal
vectors $\langle10,2,-2\rangle$ and $\langle5,1,-1\rangle$ are parallel. Choose
any point on one plane and compute its distance to the other: putting
$y=z=0$ in the first plane gives $10x=5$, so $\left(\tfrac12,0,0\right)$ is a
point of it. Then
$$D=\frac{|ax_1+by_1+cz_1+d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}=\frac{\left|5\left(\frac12\right)+1(0)-1(0)-1\right|}{\sqrt{5^2+1^2+(-1)^2}}=\frac{\sqrt3}{6}$$
