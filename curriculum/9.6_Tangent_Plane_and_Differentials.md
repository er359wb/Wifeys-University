## 1. Tangent planes (slides 2-6)

**Definition (slide 2).** Suppose a surface $S$ has equation $z=f(x,y)$, where
$f$ has continuous first partial derivatives. Let $P(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ be a point on
$S$. Let $C_1$ and $C_2$ be the curves obtained by intersecting the vertical
planes $y=y_0$ and $x=x_0$ with the surface $S$, and let $T_1$ and $T_2$ be the
tangent lines to $C_1$ and $C_2$ at $P$. Then **the tangent plane** to $S$ at $P$
is defined to be the plane that contains both tangent lines $T_1$ and $T_2$.

**Conclusion (slide 3).** If $C$ is any other curve that lies on $S$ and passes
through $P$, then its tangent line at $P$ also lies in the tangent plane. We can
think of the tangent plane to $S$ at $P$ as consisting of all possible tangent
lines at $P$ to curves that lie on $S$ and pass through $P$. The tangent plane
at $P$ is the plane that most closely approximates the surface $S$ near the
point $P$.

**Deriving the equation (slide 4).** Any plane through $P(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ has an
equation of the form $A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0$. Dividing by $C$ and letting
$a=-\dfrac AC$ and $b=-\dfrac BC$, it can be written
$$z-z_0=a(x-x_0)+b(y-y_0)$$
Suppose this represents the tangent plane at $P$. Its intersection with the plane
$y=y_0$ must be the tangent line $T_1$: setting $y=y_0$ gives
$z-z_0=a(x-x_0)$, the equation of $T_1$ in point-slope form with slope $a$, so
$a=f_x(x_0,y_0)$ (the slope of the trace from 9.3). Similarly, putting $x=x_0$
gives $b=f_y(x_0,y_0)$.

**An equation of the tangent plane (slide 5).** Suppose $f$ has continuous
partial derivatives. An equation of the tangent plane to the surface
$z=f(x,y)$ at the point $P(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ is
$$z-z_0=f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)+f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0)$$

**Example 1 (slide 5).** Find the tangent plane to the elliptic paraboloid
$z=2x^2+y^2$ at the point $(1,1,3)$.

**Example 2 (slide 6).** Find the tangent plane to the given surface at the
specified point: (1) $z=3y^2-2x^2$ at $(2,-1,-3)$; (2) $z=\sqrt{xy}$ at $(1,1,1)$;
(3) $z=x\sin(x+y)$ at $(-1,1,0)$; (4) $z=\ln(x-2y)$ at $(3,1,0)$.

## 2. Differentials (slides 7-10)

**Differentiable (slide 7).** If $z=f(x,y)$, then $f$ is **differentiable** at
$(a,b)$ if $\Delta z$ can be expressed in the form
$$\Delta z=f_x(a,b)\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\Delta y+\varepsilon_1\Delta x+\varepsilon_2\Delta y$$
where $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2\to0$ as $(\Delta x,\Delta y)\to(0,0)$. The
slide labels the first two terms $f_x\Delta x+f_y\Delta y$ as the **linear
approximation**.

**Theorem (slide 7).** If the partial derivatives $f_x$ and $f_y$ exist near
$(a,b)$ and are continuous at $(a,b)$, then $f$ is differentiable at $(a,b)$.

**Differential (slide 8).** For a differentiable function of two variables
$z=f(x,y)$, we define the **differentials** $dx$ and $dy$ to be independent
variables. Then the differential $dz$, also called the **total differential**, is
defined by
$$dz=f_x(x,y)\,dx+f_y(x,y)\,dy=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}dx+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}dy$$

**Example 3 (slide 9).** (1) If $z=f(x,y)=x^2+3xy-y^2$, find the differential
