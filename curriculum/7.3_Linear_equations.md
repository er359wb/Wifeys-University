## 1. First-order linear equations (slide 2)

A first-order linear differential equation is one that can be put into the form
$$\frac{dy}{dx}+P(x)y=Q(x)$$
where $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are continuous functions on a given interval.
Examples on the slide: $y'+xy=2\sin x$ gives $P(x)=x$, $Q(x)=2\sin x$;
$xy'+y=2x$ gives $y'+\frac1xy=2$, so $P(x)=\frac1x$, $Q(x)=2$.

A special case that can be solved directly: $xy'+y=2x\Rightarrow(xy)'=2x\Rightarrow\int(xy)'dx=\int2x\,dx\Rightarrow xy=x^2+C$.
No derivative remains; it satisfies $xy'+y=2x$ and has one arbitrary constant,
so it is the general solution.

## 2. How to solve it: the integrating factor (slides 3-4)

**Idea (slide 3).** Multiply $y'+P(x)y=Q(x)$ by an unknown function $I(x)$
(the integrating factor) chosen so that the left side becomes $(I(x)y)'$:
$$I y'+IPy=IQ\ \Rightarrow\ (Iy)'=IQ\ \Rightarrow\ \int(Iy)'dx=\int IQ\,dx\ \Rightarrow\ Iy=\int IQ\,dx+C$$
$$\Rightarrow\ y=\frac1{I(x)}\left[\int I(x)Q(x)\,dx+C\right]$$
So far the expression for $I(x)$ is unknown.

**Finding $I(x)$ (slide 4).** The product rule gives $(Iy)'=I'y+Iy'$, so we need
$IPy=I'y$, i.e. $IP=I'$, i.e. $\dfrac{I'}{I}=P$. Then
$\int\dfrac1I\,dI=\int P\,dx\Rightarrow\ln|I|=\int P(x)dx+C\Rightarrow I=Ae^{\int P(x)dx}$.
Choose only one: let $A=1$:
$$I(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,dx}$$

**Summary (slide 4).** To solve $y'+P(x)y=Q(x)$: multiply both sides by the
integrating factor $I(x)=e^{\int P(x)dx}$ and integrate both sides. The general
solution is
$$y(x)=\frac1{I(x)}\left(\int I(x)Q(x)\,dx+C\right)=e^{-\int P(x)dx}\left(\int e^{\int P(x)dx}Q(x)\,dx+C\right)$$

## 3. Examples (slides 5-8)

**Example 1 (slide 5).** Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx}+3x^2y=6x^2$. $P=3x^2$, $Q=6x^2$,
$I(x)=e^{\int3x^2dx}=e^{x^3}$. Multiplying: $e^{x^3}y'+3x^2e^{x^3}y=6x^2e^{x^3}$,
i.e. $\dfrac{d}{dx}(e^{x^3}y)=6x^2e^{x^3}$. Integrating:
$e^{x^3}y=\int6x^2e^{x^3}dx=2e^{x^3}+C$, so the general solution is
$$y=2+Ce^{-x^3}$$

**Example 2 (slide 6).** Solve the initial-value problem
$x^2y'+xy=1,\ x>0,\ y(1)=2$. Divide by the coefficient of $y'$ to get the
standard form $y'+\dfrac yx=\dfrac1{x^2}$ ($x>0$). Integrating factor
$I(x)=e^{\int\frac1xdx}=x$. Multiplying by $x$: $xy'+y=\frac1x$, i.e.
$(xy)'=\frac1x$, so $xy=\int\frac1xdx=\ln x+C$ and $y=\dfrac{\ln x+C}{x}$.
Since $y(1)=2$: $2=\dfrac{\ln1+C}{1}=C$. The solution of the initial-value
problem is $y=\dfrac{\ln x+2}{x}$.

**Example 3 (slides 7-8), an electric circuit.** A simple circuit has an
electromotive force (a battery or generator) producing a voltage $E(t)$ volts
and a current $I(t)$ amperes at time $t$, a resistor with resistance $R$ ohms
and an inductor with inductance $L$ henries. Ohm's law gives the voltage drop
across the resistor as $RI$; the drop across the inductor is $L\frac{dI}{dt}$;
Kirchhoff's law says the sum of the voltage drops equals the supplied voltage:
$$L\frac{dI}{dt}+RI=E(t)$$
Suppose $R=12\ \Omega$, $L=4$ H, the battery gives a constant 60 V and the
switch is closed at $t=0$, so $I(0)=0$. Find (a) $I(t)$; (b) the current after
1 s; (c) the limiting value of the current.
(a) $4\dfrac{dI}{dt}+12I=60,\ I(0)=0$; the general solution is $I(t)=5+Ce^{-3t}$;
with $I(0)=0$: $I(t)=5-5e^{-3t}$.
(b) $I(1)=5-5e^{-3}\approx4.75$ A.
(c) $\lim_{t\to\infty}I(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}(5-5e^{-3t})=5$.
