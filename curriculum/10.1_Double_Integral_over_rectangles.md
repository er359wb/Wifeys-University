## 1. Recall: the definite integral of one variable (slides 2-3)

If $f(x)$ is defined for $a\le x\le b$: divide $[a,b]$ into $n$ subintervals
$[x_{i-1},x_i]$ of width $\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}$; choose sample points
$x_i^*$ in each; form the Riemann sum $\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\Delta x$; take the
limit:
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^nf(x_i^*)\Delta x=\int_a^bf(x)\,dx$$
Geometric meaning (slide 3), for $f(x)\ge0$: the sum is the total area of the
approximating rectangles, and the integral is the area under $y=f(x)$ from
$a$ to $b$.

## 2. Volume under a surface over a rectangle (slides 4-6)

**Setup (slide 4).** Consider a function $f$ of two variables on a closed
rectangle
$$R=[a,b]\times[c,d]=\{(x,y)\mid a\le x\le b,\ c\le y\le d\}$$
and first suppose $f(x,y)\ge0$. Let $S$ be the solid that lies above $R$ and
under the graph of $f$:
$$S=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb R^3\mid0\le z\le f(x,y),\ (x,y)\in R\}$$
**Our goal is to find the volume of $S$.**

**Subrectangles (slide 5).** Divide $[a,b]$ into $m$ subintervals of equal
width $\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{m}$ and $[c,d]$ into $n$ subintervals of equal
width $\Delta y=\dfrac{d-c}{n}$. The subrectangles are
$$R_{ij}=[x_{i-1},x_i]\times[y_{j-1},y_j]$$
and each has area $\Delta A=\Delta x\,\Delta y$.

**Boxes and the sum (slide 6).** Approximate the part of $S$ above each
$R_{ij}$ by a thin rectangular box with base $R_{ij}$ and height
$f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)$, where $(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)$ is a sample point in
$R_{ij}$:
$$\Delta V_{ij}\approx f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A$$
Add the volumes of all the boxes:
$$V\approx\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^nf(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A$$
and take the limit:
$$V=\lim_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^nf(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A$$

## 3. The double integral (slides 7-9)

**Definition (slide 7).** The double integral of $f$ over the rectangle $R$ is
$$\iint_Rf(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\lim_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^nf(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A$$
if this limit exists (the sum is called a **double Riemann sum**). A function
$f$ is called **integrable** if the limit exists.

**Geometric interpretation (slide 8).** If $f(x,y)\ge0$, then
$\iint f(x,y)\,dA$ represents the volume of the solid $S$ that lies under the
graph of $f$ and above the rectangle $R$; a double Riemann sum is an
approximation to that volume. (The slide writes $D$ as the subscript on the
first integral, meaning the rectangle $R$.)

**Note (slide 9).** In particular, if $f$ is bounded [that is, there is a
constant $M$ with $|f(x,y)|\le M$ for all $(x,y)$ in $R$], and $f$ is
continuous there, except on a finite number of smooth curves, then $f$ is
integrable over $R$.

## 4. Example 1: estimating a volume (slides 10-11)

Estimate the volume of the solid that lies above the square $R=[0,2]\times[0,2]$
and below the elliptic paraboloid $z=16-x^2-2y^2$. Divide $R$ into four equal
squares and choose the sample point to be the upper right corner of each square
$R_{ij}$ (slide 10 shows the squares with corners $(1,1)$, $(1,2)$, $(2,1)$,
$(2,2)$), so $\Delta A=1$:
$$V\approx\sum_{i=1}^2\sum_{j=1}^2f(x_i,y_j)\Delta A=f(1,1)\Delta A+f(1,2)\Delta A+f(2,1)\Delta A+f(2,2)\Delta A=13+7+10+4=34$$
(Values: $f(1,1)=13$, $f(1,2)=7$, $f(2,1)=10$, $f(2,2)=4$.)

**Slide 11** shows the approximating boxes for finer divisions:
(a) $m=n=4$, $V\approx41.5$; (b) $m=n=8$, $V\approx44.875$;
(c) $m=n=16$, $V\approx46.46875$, with the caption "Increase the number of
squares" and the exact volume $48$. (Check by hand with iterated integrals:
$\int_0^2\!\int_0^2(16-x^2-2y^2)\,dy\,dx=48$.)

## 5. Properties (slide 12)

**Linearity of the integral:**
$$\iint_R[f(x,y)+g(x,y)]\,dA=\iint_Rf(x,y)\,dA+\iint_Rg(x,y)\,dA$$
$$\iint_Rkf(x,y)\,dA=k\iint_Rf(x,y)\,dA,\quad k\text{ a constant}$$
**Comparison:** if $f(x,y)\ge g(x,y)$ for all $(x,y)$ in $R$, then
$\iint_Rf\,dA\ge\iint_Rg\,dA$.

## 6. Iterated integrals (slides 13-14)

**Why (slide 13).** It is usually difficult to evaluate double integrals
directly from the definition. Suppose $f$ is integrable on $R=[a,b]\times[c,d]$.

- First integrate $f(x,y)$ with respect to $y$ from $y=c$ to $y=d$:
  $\displaystyle\int_c^df(x,y)\,dy$, where **$x$ is held fixed**. The result is
  a number that depends on the value of $x$. This procedure is called
  **partial integration with respect to $y$**.
- Then integrate $A(x)=\displaystyle\int_c^df(x,y)\,dy$ with respect to $x$
  from $a$ to $b$:
$$\iint_Rf(x,y)\,dA=\int_a^b\left[\int_c^df(x,y)\,dy\right]dx=\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^df(x,y)\,dy\,dx$$
This is an **iterated integral**.

**What the notation means (slide 14).**
- $\displaystyle\iint_Rf\,dA=\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^df(x,y)\,dy\,dx$ means: first
  integrate with respect to $y$ from $c$ to $d$, then with respect to $x$ from
  $a$ to $b$ (the inner integral is the one next to $f$, i.e. the **first**
  differential $dy$).
- $\displaystyle\iint_Rf\,dA=\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^bf(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ means: first
  integrate with respect to $x$ from $a$ to $b$ (holding $y$ fixed), then with
  respect to $y$ from $c$ to $d$.

(The slide states only the definitions; that both orders give the same value
for the functions in the examples below is Fubini's theorem, which is not on
the slides. It can be used to check an answer by doing the other order.)

## 7. Examples 2-4 (slides 15-17, no solutions on the slides)
