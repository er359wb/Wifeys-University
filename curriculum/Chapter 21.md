## 21.1 Electric Charge [s6–s18]

### Basics [s7–s10]

- Electric charge is **an attribute** that is as fundamental as mass.
- **Electrostatics** = the interactions between electric charges that are
  **at rest** (or nearly so).
- Figure [s8] (plastic rods + fur): (a) initially, rods neither attract nor
  repel; (b) two plastic rods rubbed with fur repel each other; (c) a plastic
  rod rubbed with fur and a glass rod rubbed with silk attract.
  → Two **negative** charges repel each other. A positive and a negative
  charge **attract**.
- Figure [s9] (glass rods + silk): (c) initially neither attract nor repel;
  (d) two glass rods rubbed with silk repel each other; (f) the fur attracts
  the plastic rod / the silk attracts the glass rod.
  → Two **positive** charges repel each other. A positive and a negative
  charge **attract**.
- **Types of charge [s10]:** there are exactly **two kinds** of electric
  charge, negative (−) and positive (+). Two positive or two negative charges
  **repel**; a positive and a negative charge **attract**.

### Caution — "like" vs "opposite" [s11]

- "**Like charges**" only means the two charges have the same algebraic sign
  (both positive or both negative).
- "**Opposite charges**" means the charges have different signs (one
  positive, one negative).

### Application: laser printer [s11–s12]

Figure [s12], steps around a rotating imaging drum:
1. Wire sprays ions onto the drum, giving it a positive charge.
2. Laser beam "writes" on the drum, leaving negatively charged areas where the
   image will be.
3. Roller applies positively charged toner to the drum; toner sticks only to
   the negatively charged areas "written" by the laser.
4. Wires spray a stronger negative charge on the paper so the toner will
   stick to it.
5. Fuser rollers heat the paper so the toner remains permanently attached.
6. Lamp discharges the drum, ready to start the process over.

### Electric charge and the structure of matter [s13–s16]

- Atoms are made of three particles: the negatively charged **electron**, the
  positively charged **proton**, and the uncharged **neutron**.
- Protons and neutrons make up the **nucleus**; electrons orbit it from a
  distance.
- Figure [s14] — lithium:
  - (a) Neutral Li: 3 protons (3+), 4 neutrons, 3 electrons (3−). Electrons
    equal protons → zero net charge.
  - (b) Positive ion Li⁺: 3 protons, 4 neutrons, 2 electrons. Fewer electrons
    than protons → positive net charge.
  - (c) Negative ion Li⁻: 3 protons, 4 neutrons, 4 electrons. More electrons
    than protons → negative net charge.
- **Atomic number** = number of protons (or electrons) in a neutral atom of
  an element.
- A **positive ion** forms by removing one or more electrons from an atom; a
  **negative ion** has gained one or more electrons. This process is called
  **ionization**.
- When total protons = total electrons in a macroscopic body, its total
  charge is zero and it is electrically **neutral**.
- "The charge of a body" always means its **net charge**.

### Conservation of charge [s17]

- **Principle of conservation of charge:** the algebraic sum of all the
  electric charges in any closed system is constant. (The net charge of a
  closed system never changes.)
- In any charging process, charge is not created or destroyed — only
  transferred from one body to another.
- Net charge = **the algebraic sum** of all the electric charges.

### Elementary charge [s18]

- The magnitude of charge of the electron or proton is a natural unit of
  charge.
- Every observable amount of charge on a macroscopic body is either zero or
  **an integer multiple (positive or negative) of this basic unit** —
  **quantization of charge**.
- The most fundamental unit of charge is the magnitude of the electron or
  proton charge, $e$:

$$e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$$

## 21.2 Conductors, Insulators, and Induced Charges [s19–s33]

### Conductors and insulators [s20–s21]

- **Conductors** permit electric charge to move easily through them.
- **Insulators** do not permit charge to move easily through them.
  - Most metals are good conductors; most non-metals are insulators.
- **Semiconductors** are intermediate between good conductors and good
  insulators.
- Figure [s21]: copper as a good conductor; glass and nylon as good
  insulators. (a) A metal ball hangs on insulating nylon threads; a copper
  wire touches a charged plastic rod — the wire conducts charge from the
  negatively charged rod to the ball. (b) A negatively charged plastic rod
  now repels the ball. (c) A positively charged glass rod attracts the ball.

### Ways to charge [s22–s24]

- **Charging insulators by rubbing:** if both objects had zero net charge
  before being rubbed together, afterwards they have net charges of equal
  magnitude and opposite sign (charge is conserved). Two insulators are
  rubbed together to separate charge.
- **Charging a conductor by contact.**
- **Grounding a conductor:** the earth is a conductor so large it acts as an
  infinite source of extra electrons or sink of unwanted electrons. The
  charge it acquires via induction is equal and opposite to the charge
  remaining on the induced body.
- **Charging a conductor by induction:** a charged body gives another body a
  charge of opposite sign **without losing any of its own charge**.
  - Excess charges that develop in a region of a body during induction are
    called **induced charges**.
  - In a metallic conductor, the **mobile charges are always negative
    electrons**.
  - In ionic solutions and ionized gases, **both positive and negative
    charges** are mobile.

### Charging a metal ball by induction [s25–s28]

- (a) Uncharged metal ball on an insulating stand.
- (b) A negatively charged rod is brought near: it repels electrons, creating
  zones of negative (electron buildup, far side) and positive (electron
  deficiency, near side) induced charge.
- (c) A wire to ground lets the electron build-up (induced negative charge)
  flow into the ground.
- (d) Wire removed; the ball now has only an electron-deficient region of
  positive charge (negative charge now in the ground).
- (e) Rod removed; electrons rearrange, ball has an overall electron
  deficiency (**net positive charge**).
- [s28] repeats the key points: induced charges; earth as infinite
  source/sink; metals → mobile electrons; ionic solutions/ionized gases →
  both signs mobile.

### Polarization [s29–s33]

- A charged body can exert forces even on objects that are **not** charged —
  the induced-charge effect.
- This is due to **polarization**: a charged object of **either** sign
  exerts an attractive force on an uncharged (neutral) insulator.
- A neutral object may have separated regions of positive and negative
  charge within it — such an object is **polarized**.
- A polarized object can **experience an electric force** even though its net
  charge is zero.
- Figures [s30–s33]:
  - (a) A charged comb picking up uncharged bits of plastic.
  - (b) Negatively charged comb: electrons in each molecule of the neutral
    insulator shift **away** from the comb, so the (+) charges in each
    molecule are closer to the comb and feel a stronger force → net force is
    attractive.
  - (c) Positively charged comb: electrons shift **toward** the comb, so the
    (−) charges are closer and feel a stronger force → net force again
    attractive.
- The charges within the molecules of an insulator can shift slightly, so a
  comb with either sign of charge attracts a neutral insulator. By Newton's
  third law, the insulator exerts an equal-magnitude attractive force on the
  comb.

## 21.3 Coulomb's Law [s34–s66]

### The law [s35–s36]

**Coulomb's law:** the magnitude of the electric force between two point
charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely
proportional to the square of the distance between them.

$$F = k\frac{|q_1||q_2|}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1||q_2|}{r^2}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$$

[note: slide s35 writes the unit of $k$ as N·m/C² — it should be N·m²/C².]

- The forces two charges exert on each other are always **along the line
  joining them** [s36].
- Figure [s36]: same-sign charges repel (forces point away from each other);
  opposite-sign charges attract (forces point toward each other). In both
  cases $\vec F_{1\text{ on }2} = -\vec F_{2\text{ on }1}$ and
  $F_{1\text{ on }2} = F_{2\text{ on }1} = k\,|q_1q_2|/r^2$.

### Example 21.1 — Electric force vs gravitational force [s37–s40]

**Problem.** An α particle (helium nucleus) has $m = 6.66\times10^{-27}$ kg and
$q = +2e = 3.2\times10^{-19}$ C. Compare the electric repulsion between two α
particles with their gravitational attraction.

**Identify and Set Up.**

$$F_e = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{r^2}, \qquad F_g = G\frac{m^2}{r^2}$$

Compare by taking the ratio.

**Execute.**

$$\frac{F_e}{F_g} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 G}\frac{q^2}{m^2} = \frac{9.0\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2}{6.67\times10^{-11}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2}\cdot\frac{(3.2\times10^{-19}\,\text{C})^2}{(6.64\times10^{-27}\,\text{kg})^2} = 3.1\times10^{35}$$

[note: the problem states $6.66\times10^{-27}$ kg but the calculation uses
$6.64\times10^{-27}$ kg; the answer rounds to $3.1\times10^{35}$ either way.]

**Evaluate.** Gravity is completely negligible compared to the electric force
here — always true for atomic and subatomic particles. But for objects the
size of a person or planet, positive and negative charges are nearly equal,
so the net electric force is usually much smaller than gravity.

### Problem-solving strategy 21.1: Coulomb's law [s41–s47]

**IDENTIFY** the relevant concepts: Coulomb's law describes the electric force
between charged particles.

**SET UP** the problem:
1. Sketch the locations of the charged particles and label each with its
   charge.
2. If the charges don't all lie on one line, set up an $xy$ coordinate
   system.
3. Identify which particle(s) you need to find the force on.

**EXECUTE** the solution:
1. For each particle that exerts a force on a particle of interest, find the
   magnitude of that force.
2. Sketch a free-body diagram of the electric-force vectors on each particle
   of interest. The force by particle 1 on particle 2 points from 2 **toward**
   1 if the charges have opposite signs, and from 2 directly **away** from 1
   if they have the same sign.
3. Use the principle of superposition to get the total force — a **vector
   sum** — on each particle of interest.
4. Use consistent units (SI): with $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 9.0\times10^{9}$
   N·m²/C², distances in meters, charges in coulombs, forces in newtons.
5. For continuous distributions of charge (along a line, over a surface,
   through a volume), the vector sum in step 3 becomes a vector integral:
   divide the distribution into infinitesimal pieces, apply Coulomb's law to
   each, and integrate. Sometimes this can be done without actual
   integration.
6. Exploit symmetry. E.g. two identical charges $q$ exert zero net force on a
   charge $Q$ midway between them, because the forces on $Q$ are equal and
   opposite.

**EVALUATE** your answer: check the numbers are reasonable; confirm the
direction of the net force agrees with "like signs repel, opposite signs
attract."

### Example 21.2 — Force between two point charges [s48–s53]

**Problem.** $q_1 = +25$ nC and $q_2 = -75$ nC are 3.0 cm apart. Find the
magnitude and direction of (a) the force $q_1$ exerts on $q_2$; (b) the force
$q_2$ exerts on $q_1$.

**Identify and Set Up.** Use Coulomb's law for the magnitude; use Newton's
third law for the force each exerts on the other.

**Execute (a).**

$$F_{1\text{ on }2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2} = (9.0\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2)\frac{(+25\times10^{-9}\,\text{C})(-75\times10^{-9}\,\text{C})}{(0.030\,\text{m})^2} = -0.019\,\text{N}$$

[note: the formula uses absolute values, so the magnitude is $+0.019$ N; the
slide's minus sign comes from plugging in the signed charges and just
reflects that the force is attractive.]

Opposite signs → attractive: the force on $q_2$ points toward $q_1$ along the
line joining them. Figure [s51]: (a) the two charges, separation $r$;
(b) free-body diagram for $q_2$: $\vec F_{1\text{ on }2}$ points left toward
$q_1$; (c) free-body diagram for $q_1$: $\vec F_{2\text{ on }1}$ points right
toward $q_2$.

**Execute (b).** Newton's third law applies to the electric force. Even
though the charges differ in magnitude, $F_{2\text{ on }1} = 0.019$ N, and its
direction is exactly opposite to the force $q_1$ exerts on $q_2$.

**Evaluate.** The force on $q_1$ points toward $q_2$, as it must, since
opposite charges attract.

### Example 21.3 — Vector addition of electric forces on a line [s54–s60]

**Problem.** On the positive $x$-axis: $q_1 = 1.0$ nC at 2.0 cm from the
origin, $q_2 = -3.0$ nC at 4.0 cm. What is the total force on
$q_3 = 5.0$ nC at the origin? Gravity is negligible.

**Identify.** Two forces act on $q_3$; add them.

**Set Up.** Target: the net force on $q_3$ = vector sum of the forces due to
$q_1$ and $q_2$ individually.

**Execute.** Free-body diagram (Fig. 21.11b): $q_3$ is **repelled** by $q_1$
(same sign) and **attracted** to $q_2$ (opposite sign).

$$F_{1\text{ on }3} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1q_3|}{r^2} = (9.0\times10^{9})\frac{(1.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{C})(5.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{C})}{(0.020\,\text{m})^2} = 1.12\times10^{-4}\,\text{N} = 112\,\mu\text{N}$$

[note: slide s57 writes $|q_1q_2|$ in the formula; it should be $|q_1q_3|$ —
the numbers plugged in are for $q_1$ and $q_3$.]

Negative $x$-component ($q_3$ pushed in $-x$ by $q_1$).

$$F_{2\text{ on }3} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_2q_3|}{r^2} = (9.0\times10^{9})\frac{(3.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{C})(5.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{C})}{(0.040\,\text{m})^2} = 8.4\times10^{-5}\,\text{N} = 84\,\mu\text{N}$$

[note: slide s58 writes $(0.020\,\text{m})^2$ in the denominator; $q_2$ is
4.0 cm away, so it should be $(0.040\,\text{m})^2$ — the 84 μN answer is
correct for 0.040 m.]

Positive $x$-component ($q_3$ pulled in $+x$ by $q_2$).

$$F_x = -112\,\mu\text{N} + 84\,\mu\text{N} = -28\,\mu\text{N}$$

No $y$- or $z$-components. Total force on $q_3$: to the **left**, magnitude
$28\,\mu\text{N} = 2.8\times10^{-5}$ N.

**Evaluate.** $q_2$ has 3× the charge of $q_1$ but is 2× as far, so by
Eq. (21.2) $F_{2\text{ on }3}$ should be $3/2^2 = 3/4$ of $F_{1\text{ on }3}$.
Indeed $84/112 = 0.75$. Direction makes sense: $\vec F_{1\text{ on }3}$ is
opposite to and larger than $\vec F_{2\text{ on }3}$, so the net force is in
the direction of $\vec F_{1\text{ on }3}$.

### Example 21.4 — Vector addition of electric forces in a plane [s61–s66]

**Problem.** Two equal positive charges $q_1 = q_2 = 2.0\,\mu\text{C}$
interact with a third charge $Q = 4.0\,\mu\text{C}$. Find the magnitude and
direction of the net force on $Q$.

Figure [s61]: $q_1$ on the $y$-axis at $y = +0.30$ m, $q_2$ at $y = -0.30$ m,
$Q$ on the $x$-axis at $x = 0.40$ m. Each of $q_1, q_2$ is 0.50 m from $Q$.
$\alpha$ is the angle between the line $q_1Q$ (or $q_2Q$) and the $x$-axis.
$\vec F_{1\text{ on }Q}$ points away from $q_1$: down and to the right.

**Identify and Set Up.** As in Example 21.3, find the force each charge
exerts on $Q$, then add them as vectors — best done with components.

**Execute.** Force on $Q$ due to $q_1$ (Coulomb's law):

$$F_{1\text{ on }Q} = (9.0\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2)\frac{(4.0\times10^{-6}\,\text{C})(2.0\times10^{-6}\,\text{C})}{(0.50\,\text{m})^2} = 0.29\,\text{N}$$

[note: slide s63 writes the denominator as "0.50 m²"; it should be
$(0.50\,\text{m})^2$.]

$\alpha$ is below the $x$-axis, so:

$$(F_{1\text{ on }Q})_x = F_{1\text{ on }Q}\cos\alpha = (0.29\,\text{N})\frac{0.40\,\text{m}}{0.50\,\text{m}} = 0.23\,\text{N}$$

$$(F_{1\text{ on }Q})_y = -F_{1\text{ on }Q}\sin\alpha = -(0.29\,\text{N})\frac{0.30\,\text{m}}{0.50\,\text{m}} = -0.17\,\text{N}$$

The lower charge $q_2$ exerts a force of the same magnitude at angle $\alpha$
**above** the $x$-axis. By symmetry its $x$-component is the same and its
$y$-component has the opposite sign:

$$F_x = 0.23\,\text{N} + 0.23\,\text{N} = 0.46\,\text{N}, \qquad F_y = -0.17\,\text{N} + 0.17\,\text{N} = 0$$

Total force on $Q$: in the $+x$-direction, magnitude 0.46 N.

**Evaluate.** The total force points neither directly away from $q_1$ nor
from $q_2$ — it's a compromise pointing away from the system of both. The
slide asks: can you see that the total force would not be in the
$+x$-direction if $q_1$ and $q_2$ were not equal, or the arrangement were not
so symmetrical?

## 21.4 Electric Field and Electric Forces [s67–s104]

### Definition [s68–s72]

- The **electric field** at a point is the electric force $\vec F_0$
  experienced by a test charge $q_0$ at that point, divided by $q_0$. (The
  field at a point = electric force per unit charge experienced by a charge
  at that point.)

$$\vec E = \frac{\vec F_0}{q_0} \qquad (21.3)$$

- SI unit: **newton per coulomb (N/C)**.
- Figures [s69–s70]: (a) bodies $A$ and $B$ exert forces on each other
  ($B$ is a test charge $q_0$ feeling $\vec F_0$); (b) remove $B$ and label its
  former position $P$; (c) body $A$ sets up an electric field $\vec E$ at
  $P$ — $\vec E = \vec F_0/q_0$ is the force per unit charge that $A$ exerts on
  a test charge at $P$.
- **Characteristics of the electric field [s71]:**
  - The field a charged body produces **exists at all points** in the region
    around it.
  - The electric force is an "action-at-a-distance" force that acts across
    empty space **without needing any matter** to transmit it.
  - The electric force on a charged body is **exerted by the electric
    field** created by other charged bodies.
- $\vec E$ is useful because it **does not depend on the charge** of the body
  the force is exerted on [s72].

### Force on a point charge in a field [s73–s74]

- The force on a test charge $q_0$ can vary from point to point, so the
  field can be different at different points.
- So Eq. (21.4) can only be used for the force on a **point charge**, not on
  large bodies where the field may vary across the body.

$$\vec F_0 = q_0\vec E \qquad (21.4)$$

- Figure [s74]: the force on a **positive** test charge points **in the
  direction** of the field; the force on a **negative** test charge points
  **opposite** to the field.

### Field of a point charge [s75–s79]

$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q|}{r^2} \qquad (21.6)$$

[note: slide s75 writes $q$ without absolute-value bars; as a magnitude it
should be $|q|$, which is how Example 21.5 uses it.]

- **Source point $S$:** where the point charge producing the field is.
- **Field point $P$:** where we're finding the field.
- **Unit vector $\hat r$:** direction along the line **from source point to
  field point**.
- Figures [s76–s77]: for a positive $q$, $\vec E$ at $P$ points directly away
  from the charge (same direction as $\hat r$). For a negative $q$, $\vec E$
  points directly toward the charge (opposite to $\hat r$).

Vector form:

$$\vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat r \qquad (21.7)$$

- The **vector field** is the infinite set of vectors associated with every
  point in space in an electric field [s78].
- Figure [s79]: a point charge produces a field at all points in space; the
  strength decreases with distance. (a) Positive charge: arrows point away
  from it. (b) Negative charge: arrows point toward it.
- When the magnitude and direction of the field are the same everywhere in a
  region, the field is **uniform** [s80].

### Example 21.5 — Electric-field magnitude for a point charge [s81–s84]

**Problem.** Magnitude of $\vec E$ at 2.0 m from $q = 4.0$ nC? (Could be any
small charged object, as long as its size is much less than the distance.)

**Identify and Set Up.** Use Eq. (21.6).

**Execute.**

$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q|}{r^2} = (9.0\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2)\frac{4.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(2.0\,\text{m})^2} = 9.0\,\text{N/C}$$

**Evaluate.** Check using $E$ = force per unit charge. Force on a test charge
$q_0$ at 2.0 m:

$$F_0 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|qq_0|}{r^2} = (9.0\times10^{9})\frac{(4.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{C})|q_0|}{(2.0\,\text{m})^2} = (9.0\,\text{N/C})|q_0|$$

$$E = \frac{F_0}{|q_0|} = 9.0\,\text{N/C}$$

$q$ is positive, so $\vec E$ points along the line from $q$ toward $q_0$
(Fig. 21.17b). The magnitude and direction of $\vec E$ do **not** depend on
the sign of $q_0$.

### Example 21.6 — Electric-field vector for a point charge [s85–s89]

**Problem.** $q = -8.0$ nC at the origin. Find $\vec E$ at $x = 1.2$ m,
$y = -1.6$ m.

**Identify and Set Up.** Use Eq. (21.7). Find the distance from source $S$
to field point $P$ and the unit vector $\hat r$ from $S$ to $P$.

**Execute.**

$$r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} = \sqrt{(1.2\,\text{m})^2 + (-1.6\,\text{m})^2} = 2.0\,\text{m}$$

$$\hat r = \frac{\vec r}{r} = \frac{x\hat\imath + y\hat\jmath}{r} = \frac{(1.2\,\text{m})\hat\imath + (-1.6\,\text{m})\hat\jmath}{2.0\,\text{m}} = 0.60\hat\imath - 0.80\hat\jmath$$

$$\vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat r = (9.0\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2)\frac{(-8.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{C})}{(2.0\,\text{m})^2}(0.60\hat\imath - 0.80\hat\jmath) = (-11\,\text{N/C})\hat\imath + (14\,\text{N/C})\hat\jmath$$

**Evaluate.** $q$ is negative, so $\vec E$ points from the field point toward
the charge — opposite to $\hat r$.

### Example 21.7 — Electron in a uniform field [s90–s99]

**Problem.** A battery connected to two large parallel conducting plates
gives a nearly uniform field between them (reason: next section; such plates
form capacitors, Chapter 24). Plates horizontal, 1.0 cm apart, 100-V battery,
$E = 1.00\times10^{4}$ N/C, $\vec E$ vertically **upward**.
- (a) An electron released from rest at the upper plate — its acceleration?
- (b) Speed and kinetic energy after travelling 1.0 cm to the lower plate?
- (c) Time taken?

Electron: charge $-e = -1.60\times10^{-19}$ C, mass $m = 9.11\times10^{-31}$ kg.

**Identify.** Concepts: field ↔ force, force ↔ acceleration, kinetic energy,
and kinematics (acceleration, distance, velocity, time). Figure [s92]: origin
$O$ at the upper plate, $+y$ upward, $\vec E$ up, force on the electron
$\vec F = -e\vec E$ down.

**Set Up.** Use Eq. (21.4) for the force and Newton's second law for the
acceleration. The field is uniform, so force and acceleration are constant —
use the constant-acceleration formulas from Chapter 3. $K = \frac12 mv^2$.

**Execute (a).** $\vec E$ is up ($+y$) but $\vec F$ is down (electron is
negative), so $F_y$ is negative and constant:

$$a_y = \frac{F_y}{m} = \frac{-eE}{m} = \frac{(-1.60\times10^{-19}\,\text{C})(1.00\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C})}{9.11\times10^{-31}\,\text{kg}} = -1.76\times10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2$$

An enormous acceleration — a 1000-kg car would need about
$2\times10^{18}$ N (about $2\times10^{14}$ tons). Gravity on the electron is
completely negligible compared to the electric force.

**Execute (b).** Starts from rest, so motion is only along $y$. Use
$v_y^2 = v_{0y}^2 + 2a_y(y - y_0)$ with $v_{0y} = 0$, $y_0 = 0$,
$y = -1.0\,\text{cm} = -1.0\times10^{-2}$ m:

$$|v_y| = \sqrt{2a_y y} = \sqrt{2(-1.76\times10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2)(-1.0\times10^{-2}\,\text{m})} = 5.9\times10^{6}\,\text{m/s}$$

Velocity is downward: $v_y = -5.9\times10^{6}$ m/s.

$$K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12(9.11\times10^{-31}\,\text{kg})(5.9\times10^{6}\,\text{m/s})^2 = 1.6\times10^{-17}\,\text{J}$$

**Execute (c).** From $v_y = v_{0y} + a_y t$:

$$t = \frac{v_y - v_{0y}}{a_y} = \frac{(-5.9\times10^{6}\,\text{m/s}) - (0\,\text{m/s})}{-1.76\times10^{15}\,\text{m/s}^2} = 3.4\times10^{-9}\,\text{s}$$

(Could also solve $y = y_0 + v_{0y}t + \frac12 a_y t^2$ for $t$.)

**Evaluate.** For subatomic particles like electrons, acceleration, speed,
kinetic energy and time take very different values from those for everyday
objects like baseballs and cars.

### Example 21.8 — An electron trajectory [s100–s104]

**Problem.** Launch an electron into the field of Example 21.7 with an
initial **horizontal** velocity $v_0$. What is the equation of its
trajectory? Figure [s100]: electron enters at the origin $O$ moving in $+x$;
$\vec E$ up; force $\vec F = -e\vec E$ down; path curves downward.

**Identify and Set Up.** Force and acceleration are constant (as in 21.7),
with no acceleration in $x$. Use Chapter 3's kinematics for 2D motion with
constant acceleration.

**Execute.** $a_x = 0$, $a_y = (-e)E/m$. At $t = 0$: $x_0 = y_0 = 0$,
$v_{0x} = v_0$, $v_{0y} = 0$. At time $t$:

$$x = v_0 t, \qquad y = \tfrac12 a_y t^2 = -\frac12\frac{eE}{m}t^2$$

Eliminating $t$:

$$y = -\frac12\frac{eE}{mv_0^2}x^2$$

**Evaluate.** This is a **parabola**, just like a projectile launched
horizontally in Earth's gravity. For a given $v_0$, the curvature depends on
$E$. Reversing the signs of the charges on the plates reverses $\vec E$, and
the electron curves **up** instead of down. So we can "steer" the electron
by varying the charges on the plates — this is how electron beams are
controlled in oscilloscopes.

## 21.5 Electric-Field Calculations [s105–s134]

### Charge densities and superposition [s106–s107]

In most realistic situations, charge is distributed over space:
- $\lambda$, **linear charge density** — charge along a line, units C/m.
- $\sigma$, **surface charge density** — charge over a surface, units C/m².
- $\rho$, **volume charge density** — charge through a volume, units C/m³.

**Principle of superposition of electric fields:** the total field at a point
$P$ is the vector sum of the fields from each point charge:

$$\vec E = \frac{\vec F_0}{q_0} = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \vec E_3 + \cdots$$

### Example 21.9 — Field of an electric dipole [s108–s121]

**Problem.** $q_1 = +12$ nC and $q_2 = -12$ nC are 0.10 m apart. (Equal
magnitude, opposite sign = an **electric dipole**; more in Section 21.7.)
Find the field from $q_1$, the field from $q_2$, and the total field at
(a) point $a$, (b) point $b$, (c) point $c$.

Figure 21.23 [s109]: both charges on the $x$-axis, $q_1$ (+) on the left,
$q_2$ (−) 10 cm to its right.
- Point $a$: between them, 6.0 cm from $q_1$ and 4.0 cm from $q_2$.
- Point $b$: 4.0 cm to the **left** of $q_1$ (so 14.0 cm from $q_2$).
- Point $c$: above the midpoint, 13.0 cm from each charge. $\alpha$ is the
  angle between each 13.0 cm line and the $x$-axis.

**Identify and Set Up.** Superposition: $\vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2$.

**Execute (a).** At $a$, $\vec E_1$ (from positive $q_1$) and $\vec E_2$
(from negative $q_2$) both point to the **right**.

$$E_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1|}{r^2} = (9.0\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2)\frac{12\times10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.060\,\text{m})^2} = 3.0\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$$

$$E_2 = (9.0\times10^{9})\frac{12\times10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.040\,\text{m})^2} = 6.8\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$$

$E_{1x} = 3.0\times10^4$ N/C, $E_{1y} = 0$; $E_{2x} = 6.8\times10^4$ N/C,
$E_{2y} = 0$.

$$(E_a)_x = (3.0 + 6.8)\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C}, \quad (E_a)_y = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec E_a = (9.8\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C})\,\hat\imath$$

**Execute (b).** At $b$, $\vec E_1$ points **left**, $\vec E_2$ points
**right**.

$$E_1 = (9.0\times10^{9})\frac{12\times10^{-9}}{(0.040\,\text{m})^2} = 6.8\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C}, \qquad E_2 = (9.0\times10^{9})\frac{12\times10^{-9}}{(0.140\,\text{m})^2} = 0.55\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C}$$

$E_{1x} = -6.8\times10^4$ N/C, $E_{2x} = 0.55\times10^4$ N/C, $y$-components
zero.

$$(E_b)_x = (-6.8 + 0.55)\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C} \;\Rightarrow\; \vec E_b = (-6.2\times10^{4}\,\text{N/C})\,\hat\imath$$

**Execute (c).** $c$ is equidistant from two equal-magnitude charges, so
$E_1 = E_2$:

$$E_1 = E_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q|}{r^2} = (9.0\times10^{9})\frac{12\times10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.13\,\text{m})^2} = 6.39\times10^{3}\,\text{N/C}$$

[note: slide s116 writes $6.39\times10^{4}$ N/C — the correct value is
$6.39\times10^{3}$ N/C, which is what s117 uses.]

The $x$-components are equal ($\cos\alpha = 5/13$):

$$E_{1x} = E_{2x} = E_1\cos\alpha = (6.39\times10^{3}\,\text{N/C})\left(\tfrac{5}{13}\right) = 2.46\times10^{3}\,\text{N/C}$$

By symmetry the $y$-components are equal and opposite and cancel:

$$(E_c)_x = 2(2.46\times10^{3}\,\text{N/C}) = 4.9\times10^{3}\,\text{N/C}, \quad (E_c)_y = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec E_c = (4.9\times10^{3}\,\text{N/C})\,\hat\imath$$

[note: slide s118 writes $2(2.46\times10^{4})$ — should be $10^{3}$.]

The slide asks: does it surprise you that the field at $c$ is parallel to
the line between the two charges?

**Evaluate [s119–s121].** Alternative: use the vector form, Eq. (21.7).
Displacement from $q_1$ to $c$ ($r = 13.0$ cm):

$$\vec r_1 = r\cos\alpha\,\hat\imath + r\sin\alpha\,\hat\jmath, \qquad \hat r_1 = \vec r_1/r = \cos\alpha\,\hat\imath + \sin\alpha\,\hat\jmath$$

$$\vec E_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{r^2}\hat r_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{r^2}(\cos\alpha\,\hat\imath + \sin\alpha\,\hat\jmath)$$

By symmetry, the unit vector from $q_2$ to $c$ has the opposite
$x$-component and the same $y$-component:

$$\vec E_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_2}{r^2}(-\cos\alpha\,\hat\imath + \sin\alpha\,\hat\jmath)$$

Since $q_2 = -q_1$:

$$\vec E_c = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{r^2}(\cos\alpha\,\hat\imath + \sin\alpha\,\hat\jmath) + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(-q_1)}{r^2}(-\cos\alpha\,\hat\imath + \sin\alpha\,\hat\jmath) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{r^2}(2\cos\alpha\,\hat\imath)$$

$$= (9.0\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2)\frac{12\times10^{-9}\,\text{C}}{(0.13\,\text{m})^2}\left[2\left(\tfrac{5}{13}\right)\right]\hat\imath = (4.9\times10^{3}\,\text{N/C})\,\hat\imath$$

### Example 21.10 — Field of a ring of charge [s122–s134]

**Problem.** A ring-shaped conductor of radius $a$ carries total charge $Q$
spread uniformly around it. Find $\vec E$ at a point $P$ on the ring's axis, a
distance $x$ from its centre.

Figure [s122]: ring in the $yz$-plane centred at $O$; $P$ on the $x$-axis. A
small segment $ds$ with charge $dQ$ at the top of the ring is a distance
$r = \sqrt{x^2+a^2}$ from $P$. Its field $d\vec E$ at $P$ points away from
the segment at angle $\alpha$ below the $x$-axis, with components $dE_x$
(along $+x$) and $dE_y$ (downward).

**Identify and Set Up.** Superposition: each bit of charge on the ring makes
a field at $P$; the target is the total field from all the bits.

**Execute.**
- Divide the ring into infinitesimal segments $ds$, each with charge $dQ$,
  acting as a point-charge source. The net field at $P$ is the sum of all the
  $d\vec E$ contributions. (This technique works for any charge distributed
  along a line or curve.)
- Symmetry: $P$ is on the ring's symmetry axis. Segments at the top and
  bottom give $d\vec E$ with the same $x$-component but opposite
  $y$-components, so their $y$-components cancel.
- Adding all such pairs, $\vec E$ has only an $x$-component (along the axis),
  no $y$- or $z$-component. So the field is described completely by $E_x$.
- Distance from a segment to $P$: $r^2 = x^2 + a^2$. So

$$dE = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dQ}{x^2+a^2}$$

- Using $\cos\alpha = x/r = x/(x^2+a^2)^{1/2}$:

$$dE_x = dE\cos\alpha = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dQ}{x^2+a^2}\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{x\,dQ}{(x^2+a^2)^{3/2}}$$

- Integrate over the ring:

$$E_x = \int\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{x\,dQ}{(x^2+a^2)^{3/2}}$$

  $x$ doesn't change as we move around the ring, so everything except $dQ$ is
  constant and comes outside the integral. $\int dQ = Q$:

$$\vec E = E_x\hat\imath = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Qx}{(x^2+a^2)^{3/2}}\hat\imath \qquad (21.8)$$

**Evaluate.**
- At the centre ($x = 0$) the field is **zero** — charges on opposite sides
  push a test charge in opposite directions and cancel in pairs.
- Far away ($x \gg a$), the denominator ≈ $x^3$, so

$$\vec E \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{x^2}\hat\imath$$

  — the same as a point charge. From far away the ring looks like a point.
- We used a symmetry argument to conclude $\vec E$ had only an $x$-component
  on the axis. Symmetry arguments will be used a lot, but only work in
  special cases. At a point in the $xy$-plane **not** on the $x$-axis, the
  argument fails and the field generally has both $x$- and $y$-components.

## 21.6 Electric Field Lines [s135–s140]

- An **electric field line** is an imaginary line or curve through a region
  of space whose **tangent** at any point is in the direction of the
  electric-field vector there. (Visualizes the field.)
- **Interpretation:**
  - The direction of $\vec E$ at any point is tangent to the field line
    through that point, in the direction of the arrows on the line. (Figure:
    a curved field line with $\vec E_P$ at $P$ and $\vec E_R$ at $R$, each
    tangent to the curve.)
  - The field is **strong** where lines are close together and **weak** where
    they are far apart.
  - More precisely: for a small surface perpendicular to the lines, the field
    magnitude is proportional to the number of lines crossing it divided by
    its area.
- **Rules for sketching field lines:**
  - Field lines can **start only on positive** charges and **end only on
    negative** charges.
  - The number of lines starting on a positive charge (or ending on a
    negative one) is **proportional to the magnitude of the charge**.
  - Field lines **never cross**.
- Figures [s139–s140]: (a) single positive charge — straight lines radiating
  outward; (b) a positive and a negative charge of equal magnitude (an
  electric dipole) — lines leave the + charge and curve round into the −
  charge; (c) two equal positive charges — lines leave both and bend away from
  each other, with no lines crossing between them.

## 21.7 Electric Dipoles [s141–s160]

### Force, torque, energy [s142–s145]

- An **electric dipole** is a pair of point charges of equal magnitude and
  opposite sign ($+q$ and $-q$) separated by distance $d$.
- In a **uniform** field (figure [s143]): $\vec F_+ = q\vec E$ on $+q$ and
  $\vec F_- = -q\vec E$ on $-q$. The dipole moment $\vec p$ points from $-q$
  to $+q$ at angle $\phi$ to $\vec E$; the perpendicular lever arm is
  $d\sin\phi$.
  - The **net force is zero**.
  - But there is **a torque** (directed into the page in the figure) that
    tends to rotate the dipole clockwise.
- Torque magnitude in a uniform field:

$$\tau = pE\sin\phi$$

  Vector form:

$$\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E$$

- The work done by $\tau$ as the dipole's direction changes gives the
  potential energy:

$$U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$$

(potential energy for a dipole in an electric field)

### Example 21.14 — Force and torque on an electric dipole [s146–s152]

**Problem.** A dipole in a uniform field of $5.0\times10^{5}$ N/C, parallel
to the plane of the figure. Charges $\pm1.6\times10^{-19}$ C, both in the
plane, separated by $0.125\,\text{nm} = 0.125\times10^{-9}$ m (typical
molecular values). Find (a) the net force on the dipole; (b) magnitude and
direction of the dipole moment; (c) magnitude and direction of the torque;
(d) the potential energy in the position shown.

Figure [s147]: $\vec E$ points right. $+q$ lower-left, $-q$ upper-right; the
line from $+q$ to $-q$ makes 35° with $\vec E$, so $\vec p$ (from $-q$ to
$+q$) makes 145° with $\vec E$.

**Identify and Set Up.** Use $\vec F = q\vec E$ for each charge to get the
net force. Eq. (21.14) gives the dipole moment, Eq. (21.16) the torque,
Eq. (21.18) the potential energy.

**Execute.**
- (a) Uniform field → forces on the two charges are equal and opposite;
  **total force is zero**.
- (b)

$$p = qd = (1.6\times10^{-19}\,\text{C})(0.125\times10^{-9}\,\text{m}) = 2.0\times10^{-29}\,\text{C·m}$$

  Direction: from the negative to the positive charge, 145° clockwise from
  the field direction.
- (c)

$$\tau = pE\sin\phi = (2.0\times10^{-29}\,\text{C·m})(5.0\times10^{5}\,\text{N/C})(\sin145^\circ) = 5.7\times10^{-24}\,\text{N·m}$$

  Right-hand rule: $\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E$ points **out of the page** —
  a counterclockwise torque that tends to align $\vec p$ with $\vec E$.
- (d)

$$U = -pE\cos\phi = -(2.0\times10^{-29}\,\text{C·m})(5.0\times10^{5}\,\text{N/C})(\cos145^\circ) = 8.2\times10^{-24}\,\text{J}$$

**Evaluate.** Dipole moment, torque, and potential energy are all tiny — but
this is a single molecule.

### Example 21.15 — Field of an electric dipole, revisited [s153–s160]

**Problem.** A dipole centred at the origin, $\vec p$ along $+y$ (Fig. 21.31).
Find an approximate expression for $\vec E$ at a point on the $y$-axis with
$y \gg d$. Use the binomial expansion
$(1+x)^n \cong 1 + nx + n(n-1)x^2/2 + \cdots$ for $|x| < 1$.

Figure [s154]: $+q$ at $y = +d/2$, $-q$ at $y = -d/2$. Field point on the
$y$-axis at distance $y - d/2$ from $+q$ and $y + d/2$ from $-q$.

**Identify.** Superposition: total field = field of $+q$ + field of $-q$.

**Set Up.** At the field point, $+q$'s field has a positive (upward)
$y$-component; $-q$'s has a negative (downward) one. Add, then approximate
for $y \gg d$.

**Execute.**

$$E_y = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{(y-d/2)^2} - \frac{1}{(y+d/2)^2}\right] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 y^2}\left[\left(1-\frac{d}{2y}\right)^{-2} - \left(1+\frac{d}{2y}\right)^{-2}\right]$$

(Same approach as Example 21.9.) When $y \gg d$, $d/2y \ll 1$. With $n = -2$
and $d/2y$ as $x$ in the binomial expansion, keep only the first two terms:

$$\left(1-\frac{d}{2y}\right)^{-2} \cong 1 + \frac{d}{y}, \qquad \left(1+\frac{d}{2y}\right)^{-2} \cong 1 - \frac{d}{y}$$

$$E_y \cong \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 y^2}\left[1 + \frac{d}{y} - \left(1 - \frac{d}{y}\right)\right] = \frac{qd}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 y^3} = \frac{p}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 y^3}$$

**Evaluate.**
- Alternative route: put the fractions over a common denominator, combine,
  and approximate the denominator $(y-d/2)^2(y+d/2)^2$ as $y^4$. (Left as a
  problem on the slides.)
  [note: slide s159 writes "$(y-d/s)^2(y+d/s)^2$ as $y^2$" — $d/s$ should be
  $d/2$, and the product $(y-d/2)^2(y+d/2)^2$ approximates to $y^4$.]
- Off the axes the expressions are more complicated, but far from a dipole in
  any direction the field drops off as $1/r^3$.
- Compare: point charge $1/r^2$; long line charge $1/r$; large sheet of
  charge — independent of $r$. An **electric quadrupole** (two equal dipoles
  with opposite orientation, a small distance apart) drops off as $1/r^4$.

## Summary [s161–s171]

**Electric charge, conductors, and insulators**
- The fundamental quantity in electrostatics is electric charge. Two kinds:
  positive and negative. Same sign repel, opposite sign attract.
- Charge is conserved: the total charge in an isolated system is constant.
- Ordinary matter is protons, neutrons, and electrons. Protons and neutrons
  in the nucleus are bound by the nuclear force; electrons surround the
  nucleus at a distance much greater than the nuclear size.
- Electric interactions are chiefly responsible for the structure of atoms,
  molecules, and solids.
- Conductors: charge moves easily. Insulators: it doesn't. Most metals are
  good conductors; most nonmetals are insulators.

**Coulomb's law**
- Force magnitude ∝ $q_1q_2$ and ∝ $1/r^2$; along the line joining the
  charges; repulsive for same sign, attractive for opposite.
- SI unit of charge: the coulomb (C).
- With several charges, the total force is the vector sum of the individual
  forces.

$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}, \qquad \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.988\times10^{9}\,\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$$

**Electric field**
- $\vec E$ (a vector) is the force per unit charge on a test charge at any
  point. A point charge's field points radially away from or toward it.

$$\vec E = \frac{\vec F_0}{q_0}, \qquad \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat r$$

**Superposition of electric fields**
- $\vec E$ of any combination of charges = vector sum of the individual
  fields.
- For a continuous distribution: divide into small elements, find each
  element's field, then vector-sum, usually by integrating.
- Distributions are described by $\lambda$ (linear), $\sigma$ (surface),
  $\rho$ (volume) charge density.

**Electric field lines**
- Tangent to a line = direction of $\vec E$ there.
- Lines per unit area (perpendicular to them) ∝ magnitude of $\vec E$.

**Electric dipoles**
- Pair of charges of equal magnitude $q$, opposite sign, separation $d$.
- Dipole moment $\vec p$: magnitude $p = qd$, direction from negative toward
  positive charge.
- In a field $\vec E$, the dipole feels torque $\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E$.
  Torque magnitude depends on the angle $\phi$ between $\vec p$ and $\vec E$;
  so does the potential energy.

$$\tau = pE\sin\phi, \qquad \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E, \qquad U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$$
