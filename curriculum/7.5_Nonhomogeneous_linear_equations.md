## 1. The equation and the complementary equation (slide 2)

A second-order **nonhomogeneous** linear differential equation with constant
coefficients has the form
$$a\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}+b\frac{dy}{dx}+cy=G(x)$$
where $a,b,c$ are constants and $G(x)$ is a continuous function. The related
homogeneous equation $ay''+by'+cy=0$ is called the **complementary equation** of
$ay''+by'+cy=G(x)$.

## 2. Structure of the general solution (slide 3)

**Theorem.** The general solution of $ay''+by'+cy=G(x)$ can be written
$$y(x)=y_p(x)+y_c(x)$$
where $y_p$ is a particular solution of $ay''+by'+cy=G(x)$ and $y_c$ is a general
solution of the complementary equation $ay''+by'+cy=0$.

**Proof on the slide.** Since $y_c$ is a general solution of the homogeneous
equation, $ay_c''+by_c'+cy_c=0$. Since $y_p$ is a particular solution,
$ay_p''+by_p'+cy_p=G(x)$. Then, by the basic rules of differentiation,
$$a(y_c+y_p)''+b(y_c+y_p)'+c(y_c+y_p)=(ay_c''+by_c'+cy_c)+(ay_p''+by_p'+cy_p)=0+G(x)=G(x)$$
(This shows that $y_c+y_p$ is a solution; the slide does not prove that every
solution has this form.)

## 3. Finding $y_p$ by the three basic cases (slide 4)

- **Case 1:** $G(x)$ is a polynomial: $y_p$ is a polynomial of the same degree
  as $G(x)$.
- **Case 2:** $G(x)$ is exponential: $y_p$ is an exponential.
- **Case 3:** $G(x)$ is trigonometric: $y_p$ is trigonometric.

## 4. Examples 1-4 (slides 5-8)

Each solution on the slides follows the same pattern: auxiliary equation, its
roots, $y_c$ (from 7.4), the form of $y_p$ with unknown coefficients, substitute
into the equation to find them, then $y=y_c+y_p$.

**Example 1 (slide 5).** $y''+y'+y=x^2$. Auxiliary equation $r^2+r+1=0$, roots
$r_{1,2}=-\frac12\pm\frac{\sqrt3}2i$,
$y_c=e^{-x/2}\!\left(C_1\cos\frac{\sqrt3}2x+C_2\sin\frac{\sqrt3}2x\right)$. Since
$G(x)=x^2$ is a polynomial of degree 2, seek $y_p=Ax^2+Bx+C$; $y_p'=2Ax+B$,
$y_p''=2A$. Substituting: $A=1$, $B=-2$, $C=0$. General solution:
$$y=e^{-x/2}\!\left(C_1\cos\tfrac{\sqrt3}2x+C_2\sin\tfrac{\sqrt3}2x\right)+x^2-2x$$

**Example 2 (slide 6).** $y''-y'=x^2$. Auxiliary equation $r^2-r=0$, roots
$r_1=0,\ r_2=1$, $y_c=C_1e^x+C_2$. Since $G(x)=x^2$ is a polynomial of degree 2,
and the highest degree on the left-hand side appears at the term $y'$, seek
$y_p$ of the form $Ax^3+Bx^2+Cx+D$: $y_p'=3Ax^2+2Bx+C$, $y_p''=6Ax+2B$. The
slide then states: substituting, $A=-\frac13$, $B=-1$, $C=2$, $D=0$; general
solution $y=C_1e^x+C_2-\frac13x^3-x^2+2x$.

**Slide error (checked by hand, confirmed against a zoomed render).** $C$ should
be $-2$, not $2$. Matching coefficients of $y''-y'=-3Ax^2+(6A-2B)x+(2B-C)=x^2$
gives $A=-\frac13$, $B=-1$, $C=-2$ (and $D$ is free, taken $0$). So the correct
general solution is
$$y=C_1e^x+C_2-\tfrac13x^3-x^2-2x$$
Check: $y_p=-\frac13x^3-x^2-2x$ gives $y_p'=-x^2-2x-2$, $y_p''=-2x-2$, and
$y_p''-y_p'=x^2$. With the slide's $+2x$ the left side would be $x^2-4$.

**Example 3 (slide 7).** $y''-2y'-3y=e^{-2x}$. Auxiliary equation
$r^2-2r-3=0$, roots $r_1=3,\ r_2=-1$, $y_c=C_1e^{-x}+C_2e^{3x}$. $G(x)=e^{-2x}$ is
exponential, so seek $y_p=Ae^{-2x}$; $y_p'=-2Ae^{-2x}$, $y_p''=4Ae^{-2x}$.
Substituting: $A=\frac15$. General solution:
$$y=C_1e^{-x}+C_2e^{3x}+\tfrac15e^{-2x}$$

**Example 4 (slide 8).** $y''+4y'+4y=\cos2x$. Auxiliary equation $r^2+4r+4=0$,
roots $r_1=r_2=-2$, $y_c=C_1e^{-2x}+C_2xe^{-2x}$. $G(x)=\cos2x$ is
trigonometric, so seek $y_p=A\cos2x+B\sin2x$; $y_p'=-2A\sin2x+2B\cos2x$,
$y_p''=-4A\cos2x-4B\sin2x$. Substituting: $A=0$, $B=\frac18$. General solution:
$$y=C_1e^{-2x}+C_2xe^{-2x}+\tfrac18\sin2x$$

## 5. Right-hand side that is a sum (slides 9-11)

**Theorem (slide 9).** The general solution of
$ay''+by'+cy=G_1(x)+G_2(x)$ can be written $y(x)=y_{p_1}(x)+y_{p_2}(x)+y_c(x)$,
where $y_{p_1}$ is a particular solution of $ay''+by'+cy=G_1(x)$, $y_{p_2}$ is a
particular solution of $ay''+by'+cy=G_2(x)$, and $y_c$ is a general solution of
$ay''+by'+cy=0$. The proof is the same linearity argument: the left side applied
to $y_{p_1}+y_{p_2}+y_c$ equals $G_1(x)+G_2(x)+0$.

**Example 5 (slide 10).** $y''+2y'+y=x-\sin x$. Auxiliary equation
$r^2+2r+1=0$, roots $r_1=r_2=-1$, $y_c=C_1e^{-x}+C_2xe^{-x}$. With $G_1=x$ and
$G_2=-\sin x$, seek the two particular solutions separately:
$y_{p_1}=Ax+B$, $y_{p_2}=C\sin x+D\cos x$. Substituting: $A=1$, $B=-2$, $C=0$,
$D=\frac12$. General solution:
$$y=C_1e^{-x}+C_2xe^{-x}+x-2+\tfrac12\cos x$$

**Example 6 (slide 11), an initial-value problem.** $y''-y=\cos x+e^{2x}$,
$y(0)=2$, $y'(0)=0$. Auxiliary equation $r^2-1=0$, roots $r_{1,2}=\pm1$,
$y_c=C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}$. With $G_1=\cos x$ and $G_2=e^{2x}$ seek the two
particular solutions separately; the slide shows only $y_{p_2}=Ce^{2x}$ and
prints "substituting: $A=0,\ B=-\frac12,\ C=\frac13$" without writing the form of
$y_{p_1}$ (the labels fit $y_{p_1}=A\sin x+B\cos x$). The general solution it
states is
$$y=C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}-\tfrac12\cos x+\tfrac13e^{2x}$$
and the solution of the initial-value problem is
$$y=\tfrac34e^x+\tfrac{17}{12}e^{-x}-\tfrac12\cos x+\tfrac13e^{2x}$$
(Checked by hand: for $y_{p_1}$ the cosine coefficient is $-\frac12$ and the sine
coefficient is $0$, since $y''-y=-2\cdot(\text{coefficient})\cos x$; $y_{p_2}$ gives
$3C=1$; and $y(0)=2$, $y'(0)=0$ give $C_1=\frac34$, $C_2=\frac{17}{12}$, matching
the slide's final answer.)

## 6. More right-hand sides (slide 12)

- **Case 4:** $G(x)=e^{kx}P(x)$, where $P(x)$ is an $n$th-degree polynomial: the
  particular solution is $y_p=e^{kx}Q(x)$, where $Q(x)$ is an $n$th-degree
  polynomial.
- **Case 5:** $G(x)=e^{kx}P(x)\cos mx$ or $G(x)=e^{kx}P(x)\sin mx$: the particular
  solution is $y_p=e^{kx}Q(x)\cos mx+e^{kx}R(x)\sin mx$, where $Q$ and $R$ are
  $n$th-degree polynomials.

**Example 6 again (slide 12; the deck reuses the number).** Solve the
"initial-value problem" $y''+y=e^{2x}\cos3x$. (The slide gives no initial
conditions and no solution.)

**Teaching note (not on the slides).** The slides only handle the case where the
guess for $y_p$ is not already a solution of the complementary equation (Example 2
adjusts the polynomial degree because $r=0$ is a root). The general rule: if a
term of the guess for $y_p$ already appears in $y_c$, multiply the guess by $x$
(or $x^2$ for a repeated root). This matters for Exercise 1 (2)(b)
$y''+9y'=1$, where $r=0$ is a root, so $y_p=Ax$.
