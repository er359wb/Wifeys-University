## 1. Partial derivatives of a function of two variables (slides 2-6)

**Definition w.r.t. $x$ (slide 2).** If $f$ is a function of two variables $x$
and $y$, let only $x$ vary while $y$ is fixed, $y=b$ ($b$ a constant). Then we
have a function of the single variable $x$: $g(x)=f(x,b)$. If $g$ has a
derivative at $a$, we call it the **partial derivative of $f$ with respect to
$x$ at $(a,b)$** and denote it $f_x(a,b)$:
$$f_x(a,b)=g'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{g(a+h)-g(a)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h}$$

**Definition w.r.t. $y$ (slide 3).** Similarly, $f_y(a,b)$ is obtained by
keeping $x$ fixed, $x=a$, and finding the ordinary derivative at $b$ of
$G(y)=f(a,y)$:
$$f_y(a,b)=G'(b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}{h}$$
If the point $(a,b)$ is allowed to vary, $f_x$ and $f_y$ become functions of two
variables:
$$f_x(x,y)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h,y)-f(x,y)}{h},\qquad f_y(x,y)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x,y+h)-f(x,y)}{h}$$

**Notation (slide 4).** If $z=f(x,y)$:
$$f_x(x,y)=f_x=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x,y)=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=f_1=D_1f=D_xf$$
$$f_y(x,y)=f_y=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f(x,y)=\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=f_2=D_2f=D_yf$$

**Rule for finding the partial derivatives of $z=f(x,y)$ (slide 4).**
1. To find $f_x$, regard $y$ as a constant and differentiate $f(x,y)$ with
   respect to $x$.
2. To find $f_y$, regard $x$ as a constant and differentiate $f(x,y)$ with
   respect to $y$.

**Example 1 (slide 5).** If $z=f(x,y)=2x^3y+3xy-x^2+y^2+2$, find $f_x(1,2)$ and
$f_y(-1,0)$. Holding $y$ constant: $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}=f_x=6x^2y+3y-2x$,
so $f_x(1,2)=6\cdot1^2\cdot2+3\cdot2-2\cdot1=16$. Holding $x$ constant:
$\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}=f_y=2x^3+3x+2y$, so
$f_y(-1,0)=2(-1)^3+3(-1)+2\cdot0=-5$.

**Example 2 (slide 6).** (a) If $z=f(x,y)=4-x^2-4y^2$, find $f_x(1,1)$ and
$f_y(1,1)$: $f_x=-2x$, so $f_x(1,1)=-2$; $f_y=-8y$, so $f_y(1,1)=-8$.
(b) $z=\sin\dfrac{x}{1+y}$, find $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x},\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$:
$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=\cos\frac{x}{1+y}\cdot\left(\frac{x}{1+y}\right)'_x=\cos\frac{x}{1+y}\cdot\frac1{1+y}$$
$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=\cos\frac{x}{1+y}\cdot\left(\frac{x}{1+y}\right)'_y=\cos\frac{x}{1+y}\cdot\frac{-x}{(1+y)^2}$$

## 2. Geometric interpretation (slides 7-8)

$z=f(x,y)$ represents a surface $S$. If $f(a,b)=c$, then the point $P(a,b,c)$
lies on $S$. Fixing $y=b$ restricts attention to the curve $C_1$ in which the
vertical plane $y=b$ intersects $S$; that is, $C_1$ is the trace of $S$ in the
plane $y=b$. The vertical plane $x=a$ intersects $S$ in a curve $C_2$. Both
curves pass through $P$.

$C_1:g(x)=f(x,b)$, so the slope of its tangent $T_1$ at $P$ is
$g'(a)=f_x(a,b)$. $C_2:G(y)=f(a,y)$, so the slope of its tangent $T_2$ at $P$
is $G'(b)=f_y(a,b)$.

Thus the partial derivatives $f_x(a,b)$ and $f_y(a,b)$ are the **slopes of the
tangent lines at $P(a,b,c)$ to the traces $C_1$ and $C_2$ of $S$ in the planes
$y=b$ and $x=a$**.

## 3. More than two variables (slides 9-10)

If $u=f(x,y,z)$, the partial derivative with respect to $x$ is
$$f_x(x,y,z)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h,y,z)-f(x,y,z)}{h}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=f_x=f_1$$
obtained by regarding $y$ and $z$ as constants and differentiating $f(x,y,z)$
with respect to $x$. In general, if $u=f(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)$, then
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_1,\dots,x_{i-1},x_i+h,x_{i+1},\dots,x_n)-f(x_1,\dots,x_i,\dots,x_n)}{h}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=f_{x_i}=f_i$$
obtained by regarding all the other variables as constants and differentiating
with respect to $x_i$.

**Example 3 (slide 10).** If $f(x,y,z)=e^{xy}\ln z$, find $f_x,f_y,f_z$.
Holding $y$ and $z$ constant: $f_x=ye^{xy}\ln z$; similarly $f_y=xe^{xy}\ln z$
and $f_z=\dfrac{e^{xy}}{z}$.

## 4. Higher derivatives (slides 11-14)

If $z=f(x,y)$, then $f_x$ and $f_y$ are also functions of two variables, so we
can differentiate them again. $(f_x)_x,(f_x)_y,(f_y)_x,(f_y)_y$ are the **second
partial derivatives** of $f$. Notation (slide 11):
$$(f_x)_x=f_{xx}=f_{11}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}=\frac{\partial^2z}{\partial x^2}\qquad
(f_y)_y=f_{yy}=f_{22}=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}=\frac{\partial^2z}{\partial y^2}$$
$$(f_x)_y=f_{xy}=f_{12}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\,\partial x}=\frac{\partial^2z}{\partial y\,\partial x}$$
$$(f_y)_x=f_{yx}=f_{21}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x\,\partial y}=\frac{\partial^2z}{\partial x\,\partial y}$$
The slide stresses "**the order is reversed**": in $f_{xy}$ you differentiate
first with respect to $x$, then $y$; in $\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\,\partial x}$
the same order is written right to left.

**Example 4 (slide 12).** If $f(x,y)=x^3+x^2y^2-2y^2$, find $f_{xx},f_{xy},f_{yx},f_{yy}$.
$f_x=3x^2+2xy^2$, $f_y=2x^2y-4y$. So $f_{xx}=(3x^2+2xy^2)_x=6x+2y^2$;
$f_{xy}=(3x^2+2xy^2)_y=4xy$; $f_{yx}=(2x^2y-4y)_x=4xy$;
$f_{yy}=(2x^2y-4y)_y=2x^2-4$. The slide remarks that $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$ "is not just
a coincidence": the mixed partial derivatives are equal for most functions that
one meets in practice.

**Clairaut's Theorem (slide 13).** Suppose $f$ is defined on a disk $D$ that
contains the point $(a,b)$. If the functions $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ are both
continuous on $D$, then
$$f_{xy}(a,b)=f_{yx}(a,b)$$
(Discovered by the French mathematician Alexis Clairaut, 1713-1765.) Partial
derivatives of order 3 or higher can also be defined:
$f_{xxy}=(f_{xx})_y=f_{112}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}\right)=\dfrac{\partial^3f}{\partial y\,\partial x^2}$, and
$f_{xxy}=f_{xyx}=f_{yxx}$ if these functions are continuous.

**Example 5 (slide 14).** If $f(x,y,z)=\sin(3x+yz)$, find $f_{xxyz}$. (No
solution on the slide.)
