## 1. Vectors (slides 2-4)

**Definition.** A *vector* is a quantity (such as displacement, velocity or
force) that has both **magnitude** and **direction**.

**Representing a vector.** Drawn as an arrow (directed line segment). The
*length* of the arrow represents the magnitude; the arrow *points in the
direction* of the vector.

**Denoting a vector.** Either a boldface letter ($\mathbf v$) or an arrow above
the letter ($\vec v$). If the tail (initial point) is $A$ and the tip (terminal
point) is $B$, write $\vec v=\vec{AB}$.

**Equivalent (equal) vectors.** $\vec u=\vec v$ if they have the same length
and the same direction, even if they are in different positions.

**Zero vector.** $\vec 0$ has length 0 and is the only vector with no specific
direction.

## 2. Combining Vectors (slides 5-11)

**Displacements (slide 5).** A particle moves from $A$ to $B$ (displacement
vector $\vec{AB}$), then changes direction and moves from $B$ to $C$
(displacement vector $\vec{BC}$). The combined effect is a move from $A$ to
$C$:
$$\vec{AC}=\vec{AB}+\vec{BC}$$

**Vector addition, Triangle Law (slide 6).** Move $\vec v$ so its tail
coincides with the tip of $\vec u$. Then $\vec u+\vec v$ is the vector from the
initial point of $\vec u$ to the terminal point of $\vec v$.

**Parallelogram Law (slide 7).** Place $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ so they start at
the same point. Then $\vec u+\vec v$ lies along the diagonal of the
parallelogram with $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ as sides. Shown on the slide:
$\vec u+\vec v=\vec v+\vec u$.

**Example 1 (slide 8).** Draw the sum of the vectors $\vec a$ and $\vec b$
shown. Way 1: translate $\vec b$ so its tail is at the tip of $\vec a$ (a copy
of $\vec b$ with the same length and direction), then draw $\vec a+\vec b$.
Way 2: place $\vec b$ so it starts where $\vec a$ starts, and construct
$\vec a+\vec b$ with the Parallelogram Law.

**Scalar multiplication (slide 9).** If $c$ is a scalar and $\vec v$ is a
vector, $c\vec v$ is the vector whose length is $|c|$ times the length of
$\vec v$ and whose direction is the same as $\vec v$ if $c>0$ and opposite to
$\vec v$ if $c<0$. If $c=0$ or $\vec v=\vec 0$, then $c\vec v=\vec 0$.
(Figure: $\tfrac12\vec u,\ 2\vec u,\ \vec u,\ -\vec u,\ -2.5\vec u$.)

**Theorem (slide 9).** Two nonzero vectors $\vec u,\vec v$ are parallel
$\iff \vec v=\lambda\vec u$ for some scalar $\lambda$.

**Negative vector and subtraction (slide 10).** $(-1)\vec v=-\vec v$ has the
same length as $\vec v$ but points in the opposite direction; it is called the
negative of $\vec v$. Subtraction:
$$\vec u-\vec v=\vec u+(-1)\vec v$$

**Example 2 (slide 11).** Draw $\vec a-2\vec b$. First draw $-2\vec b$
(opposite direction to $\vec b$, twice as long). Next place it with its tail at
the tip of $\vec a$. Finally use the Triangle Law to draw
$\vec a+(-2\vec b)$.

## 3. Components of Vectors (slides 12-17)

**Components (slide 12).** Put the initial point of $\vec a$ at the origin. If
the terminal point has coordinates $(a_1,a_2)$ (plane) or $(a_1,a_2,a_3)$
(space), these coordinates are the *components* of $\vec a$:
$\vec a=\langle a_1,a_2\rangle$ or $\vec a=\langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle$.

**Position vector (slides 13-14).** The representation $\vec{OP}$ from the
origin to the point $P(x,y)$ is called the *position vector* of $P$:
$\vec{OP}=\langle x,y\rangle$. Example on the slide: $P(3,2)$ has
$\vec{OP}=\langle3,2\rangle$; the figure draws many equal copies of this
vector in different places. In space, $P(a_1,a_2,a_3)$ has position vector
$\langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle$.

**Representation of any vector (slide 14).** Given $A(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ and
$B(x_2,y_2,z_2)$, the vector $\vec a=\vec{AB}$ is
$$\vec a=\langle x_2-x_1,\ y_2-y_1,\ z_2-z_1\rangle$$

**Standard basis vectors (slide 15).** $P(1,0,0)$, $Q(0,1,0)$, $R(0,0,1)$:
$\vec{OP}=\langle1,0,0\rangle=\vec i$, $\vec{OQ}=\langle0,1,0\rangle=\vec j$,
$\vec{OR}=\langle0,0,1\rangle=\vec k$.

**Example 3 (slide 16).** Find the vector represented by the directed line
segment with initial point $A(2,-3,4)$ and terminal point $B(-2,1,1)$.
$$\vec a=\langle-2-2,\ 1-(-3),\ 1-4\rangle=\langle-4,4,-3\rangle$$

**Length of a vector (slides 16-17).** The magnitude (length) of $\vec v$ is
the length of any of its representations; written $|\vec v|$ or $\|\vec v\|$.
For $\vec a=\vec{OP}=\langle a_x,a_y,a_z\rangle$, the distance formula gives
$$|\vec a|=\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2+a_z^2}$$

**Example 4 (slide 17).** Find the magnitude of the vector with initial point
$A(2,-3,4)$ and terminal point $B(-2,1,1)$. $\vec{AB}=\langle-4,4,-3\rangle$, so
$$|\vec{AB}|=\sqrt{(-4)^2+4^2+(-3)^2}=\sqrt{41}$$

## 4. Algebraic Vectors (slides 18-24)

**Slide 18.** For $\vec{OA}=\langle a_x,a_y,a_z\rangle$ (the box in the
figure, with corners $P(a_x,0,0)$, $Q(0,a_y,0)$, $R(0,0,a_z)$,
$A(a_x,a_y,a_z)$): $\vec{OP}=\langle a_x,0,0\rangle=a_x\vec i$,
$\vec{OQ}=\langle0,a_y,0\rangle=a_y\vec j$,
$\vec{OR}=\langle0,0,a_z\rangle=a_z\vec k$, and
$\vec{OA}=\vec{OP}+\vec{OQ}+\vec{OR}$, so
$$\vec{OA}=\langle a_x,a_y,a_z\rangle=a_x\vec i+a_y\vec j+a_z\vec k$$

**Adding (slide 19).** For $\vec a=\langle a_x,a_y,a_z\rangle$ and
$\vec b=\langle b_x,b_y,b_z\rangle$:
$$\vec a+\vec b=\langle a_x+b_x,\ a_y+b_y,\ a_z+b_z\rangle$$
To add algebraic vectors, add their components.

**Subtracting and scaling (slide 20).** To subtract vectors, subtract
components: $\vec a-\vec b=\langle a_x-b_x,\ a_y-b_y,\ a_z-b_z\rangle$. To
multiply a vector by a scalar, multiply each component by that scalar:
$\lambda\vec a=\langle\lambda a_x,\lambda a_y,\lambda a_z\rangle$.

**Example 5 (slide 21).** $\vec a=\langle4,0,3\rangle$,
$\vec b=\langle-2,1,5\rangle$. Find $|\vec a|$, $\vec a+\vec b$,
$\vec a-\vec b$, $3\vec b$, $2\vec a+5\vec b$. (No solution on the slide.)

**Unit vector (slides 21-22).** A unit vector is a vector whose length is 1
($|\vec i|=|\vec j|=|\vec k|=1$). If $\vec a\neq\vec 0$, the unit vector with
the same direction as $\vec a$ is
$$\vec u=\frac{1}{|\vec a|}\vec a=\frac{\vec a}{|\vec a|}$$
Why (slide 22): let $c=1/|\vec a|$, a positive scalar. Then $\vec u=c\vec a$
has the same direction as $\vec a$, and
$|\vec u|=|c\vec a|=|c||\vec a|=\frac{1}{|\vec a|}|\vec a|=1$. (The slide writes
the unit vector with the symbol $\upsilon$.)

**Properties of vectors (slide 23).** If $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c$ are vectors in
$V_n$ and $c,d$ are scalars (the slide reuses the letter $c$ for both, as
printed):
1. $\vec a+\vec b=\vec b+\vec a$
2. $\vec a+(\vec b+\vec c)=(\vec a+\vec b)+\vec c$
3. $\vec a+\vec 0=\vec a$
4. $\vec a+(-\vec a)=\vec 0$
5. $c(\vec a+\vec b)=c\vec a+c\vec b$
6. $(c+d)\vec a=c\vec a+d\vec a$
7. $(cd)\vec a=c(d\vec a)$
8. $1\vec a=\vec a$

**Example 6 (slide 24).** Find the unit vector in the direction of the vector
$2\vec i-\vec j-2\vec k$.

**Example 7 (slide 24).** The points $A(1,-1,2)$, $B(3,1,4)$, $C(-1,0,1)$; $D$
is the midpoint of segment $AB$. Find $|\vec{CD}|$, $2\vec{AD}-\vec{BC}$, and
the unit vector of $2\vec{BD}+\vec{AC}$. (No solution on the slide.)
