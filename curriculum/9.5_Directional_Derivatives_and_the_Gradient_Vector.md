## 1. The directional derivative (slides 2-6)

**Motivation (slide 2).** For $z=f(x,y)$ the partial derivatives
$f_x(x_0,y_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x_0+h,y_0)-f(x_0,y_0)}{h}$ give the rate of
change of $z$ in the $x$-directions ($\vec i$ and $-\vec i$), and
$f_y(x_0,y_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x_0,y_0+h)-f(x_0,y_0)}{h}$ the rate of change
in the $y$-directions ($\vec j$ and $-\vec j$). Now we want the rate of change of
$z$ at $(x_0,y_0)$ in the direction of an **arbitrary unit vector**
$\vec u=\langle a,b\rangle$ (picture: $\vec u$ at angle $\theta$ with components
$\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$).

**Geometry (slides 3-4).** Consider the surface $S$: $z=f(x,y)$ and
$z_0=f(x_0,y_0)$, so $P(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ lies on $S$. The vertical plane through $P$
in the direction of $\vec u$ intersects $S$ in a curve $C$. The slope of the
tangent line $T$ to $C$ at $P$ is the rate of change of $z$ in the direction of
$\vec u$. Let $Q(x,y,z)$ be another point on $C$ and $P',Q'$ the projections of
$P,Q$ on the $xy$-plane. Then $\overrightarrow{P'Q'}\parallel\vec u$, so
$\overrightarrow{P'Q'}=h\vec u=\langle ha,hb\rangle$ for some scalar $h$, i.e.
$x-x_0=ha$, $y-y_0=hb$, and
$$\frac{\Delta z}{h}=\frac{z-z_0}{h}=\frac{f(x_0+ha,\,y_0+hb)-f(x_0,y_0)}{h}$$

**Definition (slide 5).** If $h\to0$ we obtain the rate of change of $z$ (with
respect to distance) in the direction of $\vec u$. The **directional derivative**
of $f$ at $(x_0,y_0)$ in the direction of a unit vector $\vec u=\langle a,b\rangle$ is
$$D_{\vec u}f(x_0,y_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+ha,\,y_0+hb)-f(x_0,y_0)}{h}$$
if this limit exists.

**Comparison (slide 6).** $f_x(x_0,y_0)=D_{\vec i}f(x_0,y_0)$ with $\vec i=\langle1,0\rangle$ and
$f_y(x_0,y_0)=D_{\vec j}f(x_0,y_0)$ with $\vec j=\langle0,1\rangle$. In other words, the
partial derivatives of $f$ with respect to $x$ and $y$ are just special cases of
the directional derivative.

## 2. The formula and Examples 1-3 (slides 7-10)

**Theorem (slides 7 and 11, same statement).** If $f$ is a differentiable
function of $x$ and $y$, then $f$ has a directional derivative in the direction
of any unit vector $\vec u=\langle a,b\rangle$ and
$$D_{\vec u}f(x,y)=f_x(x,y)\,a+f_y(x,y)\,b$$

**Example 1 (slide 8).** Find the directional derivative $D_{\vec u}f(x,y)$ if
$f(x,y)=x^3-3xy+4y^2$ and $\vec u$ is the unit vector given by angle
$\theta=\dfrac\pi6$. What is $D_{\vec u}f(1,2)$? (The slide has a "Solution" box
but no worked solution.)

**Example 2 (slide 9).** Find $D_{\vec u}f(x,y)$ if (a) $f(x,y)=ye^{-x}$ at
$(0,4)$, $\theta=\dfrac{2\pi}3$; (b) $f(x,y)=e^x\cos y$ at $(0,0)$,
$\theta=\dfrac\pi4$; and $\vec u$ is the unit vector given by angle $\theta$.

**Example 3 (slide 10).** Find $D_{\vec u}f(x,y)$ if $f(x,y)=\sqrt{xy}$ at the
point $P(2,8)$ in the direction $\overrightarrow{PQ}$, where $Q(5,4)$. (Note:
$\overrightarrow{PQ}$ is not a unit vector; normalize it first.)

## 3. The gradient vector (slides 12-16)

**Slide 12.** The directional derivative can be written as a dot product of two
vectors:
$$D_{\vec u}f(x,y)=f_xa+f_yb=\langle f_x,f_y\rangle\cdot\langle a,b\rangle=\langle f_x,f_y\rangle\cdot\vec u$$
The vector $\langle f_x,f_y\rangle$ is called **the gradient of $f$**, written
$\operatorname{grad}f$ or $\nabla f$. It occurs not only in computing directional
derivatives but in many other contexts as well.

**Definition (slide 13).** If $f$ is a function of two variables $x$ and $y$,
the gradient of $f$ is the vector function defined by
$$\nabla f=\langle f_x(x,y),f_y(x,y)\rangle=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\vec i+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\vec j$$

**Example 4 (slide 14).** Find the gradient vector of $f(x,y)=\sin x+e^{xy}$.

**Slide 15.** With this notation,
$$D_{\vec u}f(x,y)=f_xa+f_yb=\langle f_x,f_y\rangle\cdot\vec u=\nabla f(x,y)\cdot\vec u$$
This expresses the directional derivative in the direction of $\vec u$ as the
**scalar projection of the gradient vector onto $\vec u$** (valid because $\vec u$
is a unit vector; compare $\operatorname{comp}_{\vec u}\vec b$ in 8.3).

**Example 5 (slide 16).** Find the directional derivative of $f(x,y)=x^2y^3-4y$
at the point $(2,-1)$ in the direction of the vector $\vec v=2\vec i+5\vec j$.
(Note: $\vec v$ is not a unit vector; use $\vec v/|\vec v|$.)

## 4. Three variables (slides 17-21)

**Slide 17.** For functions of three variables we can define directional
derivatives in a similar manner; $D_{\vec u}f(x,y,z)$ can be interpreted as the
rate of change of the function $f(x,y,z)$ in the direction of a unit vector
$\vec u$.

**Definition (slide 18).** The directional derivative of $f$ at $(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ in
the direction of a unit vector $\vec u=\langle a,b,c\rangle$ is
$$D_{\vec u}f(x_0,y_0,z_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+ha,\,y_0+hb,\,z_0+hc)-f(x_0,y_0,z_0)}{h}$$
if this limit exists.

**Theorem (slide 19).** If $f$ is a differentiable function of $x$, $y$ and $z$,
then $f$ has a directional derivative in the direction of any unit vector
$\vec u=\langle a,b,c\rangle$ and
$$D_{\vec u}f(x,y,z)=f_x(x,y,z)\,a+f_y(x,y,z)\,b+f_z(x,y,z)\,c$$

**Gradient (slide 20).** If $f$ is a function of three variables, the gradient of
$f$ is the vector function
$$\nabla f=\langle f_x,f_y,f_z\rangle=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\vec i+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\vec j+\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\vec k$$
and again $D_{\vec u}f=\nabla f\cdot\vec u$.

**Example 6 (slide 21).** Find the gradient vector of $f(x,y,z)=x\sin(yz)$ and the
directional derivative at the point $(1,3,0)$ in the direction of the vector
$\vec v=\vec i+2\vec j-\vec k$. (Again $\vec v$ is not a unit vector.)

## 5. Maximizing the directional derivative (slides 22-25)

**Question (slides 22-23).** In which of these directions does $f$ change fastest,
and what is the maximum rate of change?

**Derivation (slide 23).** At $(x_0,y_0)$ in some direction $\vec u=\langle a,b\rangle$
with $|\vec u|=1$:
$$D_{\vec u}f=f_xa+f_yb=\langle f_x,f_y\rangle\cdot\langle a,b\rangle=\nabla f\cdot\vec u=|\nabla f|\,|\vec u|\cos\theta=|\nabla f|\cos\theta$$
where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\langle a,b\rangle$. The maximum
value is $|\nabla f|$, attained when $\theta=0$. So the maximum of
$D_{\vec u}f(x_0,y_0)$ is
$$|\nabla f(x_0,y_0)|=\sqrt{f_x^2(x_0,y_0)+f_y^2(x_0,y_0)}$$
and it occurs when $\cos(\widehat{\nabla f,\vec u})=1$, i.e. the angle between
$\nabla f$ and $\vec u$ is $0$.

**Theorem (slide 24).** Suppose $f$ is a differentiable function of two or three
variables. The maximum value of the directional derivative $D_{\vec u}f$ is
$|\nabla f|$, and it occurs when $\vec u$ has the same direction as the gradient
vector $\nabla f$.

**Example 7 (slide 24).** (1) If $f(x,y)=xe^y$, find the rate of change of $f$ at
the point $P(2,0)$ in the direction from $P(2,0)$ to $Q\!\left(\tfrac12,2\right)$.
(2) In what direction does $f$ have the maximum rate of change? What is the
maximum value?

**Example 8 (slide 25).** $T(x,y,z)=\dfrac{80}{1+x^2+2y^2+3z^2}$. In which
direction does $T$ have the fastest increase at the point $P(1,1,-2)$? What is
the maximum rate of change?
